The Passive Turn

The passive adds no new tense to English. The same sixteen stay exactly where they were — the sentence simply turns around, and the thing that the action is done to becomes the subject. One formula covers all of it: be + V3. Everything else is tense knowledge you already have, applied to the verb be.

doer — who does it object — who it is done to be + V3 — the whole formula
Part 000:00

What the passive is

The passive is not a new tense and not a new meaning. It is a different order of roles in the same sentence. The action stays the same; what changes is who gets put first and made the subject.

Why it exists. English word order is rigid: whoever stands first is the sentence's main character. If the character of your story is not the doer but the thing being acted on, the passive is the only way to put it in front.

Four reasons to choose it. The doer is unknown (My bike was stolen — by whom, I have no idea). The doer is unimportant or obvious (He was arrested — by the police, clearly). The doer is being hidden on purpose (Mistakes were made — by whom, nobody wants to say). Or the object is simply the topic and you do not want to switch away from it.

The one hard limit. The passive works only with transitive verbs — ones that take an object. You cannot build it from sleep, come, happen or arrive: there is nothing to promote to subject.

A one-phrase test

If you can ask "what?" or "whom?" after the verb, a passive is possible. read what? — a book → The book was read.

If you cannot, there is no passive. You cannot "happen something", which is why The accident was happened is badly wrong. It is simply The accident happened.

Part 104:10

One formula for every tense

There is exactly one thing to learn: be + V3. Everything else is what you already know about tenses, applied to the verb be.

The mechanics in two steps. Step one: take the tense the active verb was in. Step two: put the verb be into that tense, turn the main verb into its third form (V3) and never touch it again — it is frozen from then on.

Watch it work on a single verb: does → is done, is doing → is being done, has done → has been done, did → was done, will do → will be done. Only be moves. The word done stands still in all eight forms.

Negatives and questions are built as usual, off the first auxiliary: is not done, has not been done, Was it done?, Will it be done? There is never any do / does / did in the passive — it already has an auxiliary of its own.

Part 208:00

The eight working forms

The timeline is exactly the same as in the active: where an action sits on the axis does not depend on which side you look at it from. So these diagrams read the same way — blue marks the action the subject undergoes.

Present · 01

Present Simple Passive

it gets done — generally, regularly, always
+it is done · they are done
−is not (isn't) / are not (aren't) done
?Is it done? · Are they done?
What it means

The most common passive form of all. It describes how things work: how something is normally done, how a process is arranged, what the rules are.

Hence its natural home — recipes, technical descriptions, rules, notices. The doer is unnecessary there: what matters is not who, but what happens.

  • Regular, repeated actions on the object
  • Rules, instructions, process descriptions
  • Widely known facts
  • Statements where the doer is "people in general"
Active → passive
  • activeThey clean the office every day.
    passiveThe office is cleaned every day.
    Who cleans it does not matter, so no by is needed.
  • activeThey make these cars in Japan.
    passiveThese cars are made in Japan.
    The classic label on a product.
  • passiveEnglish is spoken all over the world.
    The doer is "everyone", which is the same as nobody in particular.
  • passiveIs breakfast included in the price?
    In a question is moves to the front; included does not change.
The Native Mental Trigger

The receiver is the topic, the doer is people in general or beside the point, and the action is a general rule, habit or how a process normally works. This is the timeless passive of instructions, facts and notices — no now in it.

The thought: “This is simply how it's done, as a rule — put the receiver first, drop the doer.”
When a native reaches for it — each with an example
  • How a process normally worksThe bins are emptied every Monday.
  • Rules and instructionsShoes must be removed at the door.
  • Widely known factsEnglish is spoken here.
  • Doer = people in generalHe is called 'the professor'.
Signal words — each in a sentence
  • every weekThe report is reviewed every week.
  • always / usuallyMistakes are always corrected in red.
  • generally / normallyThis dish is normally served cold.
Present · 02

Present Continuous Passive

it is being done right now
+it is being done
−isn't / aren't being done
?Is it being done?
What it means

This is where being appears — and where most people stumble. Learn the chain whole: is being done. Without being you get a different tense; without is you get no sentence at all.

The crucial contrast: The window is broken means the window is in a broken state. The window is being broken means someone is smashing it right now — a process. The first describes a result, the second an action in progress.

  • A process on the object at the moment of speaking
  • A temporary, ongoing situation
  • Work in progress: being built, repaired, discussed
Active → passive
  • activeThey are repairing the road now.
    passiveThe road is being repaired now.
    are repairing → is being repaired: be goes into Continuous, the verb into V3.
  • passiveYour order is being processed.
    The standard line in interfaces and emails.
  • passiveThe problem is being discussed right now.
    Compare: The problem is discussed in chapter 4 — a general fact, not a process.
The Native Mental Trigger

The receiver is the focus and the action is in progress on it right now, unfolding as you speak; the doer is unknown or simply not the point.

The thought: “It's happening to it at this moment — is/are being + V3.”
When a native reaches for it — each with an example
  • Action in progress on the object nowThe road is being repaired.
  • A temporary current processMy car is being serviced this week.
Signal words — each in a sentence
  • now / right nowThe results are being counted right now.
  • at the momentThe system is being upgraded at the moment.
  • Look! / Listen!Look — the house is being painted.
Present · 03

Present Perfect Passive

it has been done — and it matters now
+it has been done · they have been done
−hasn't / haven't been done
?Has it been done?
What it means

The passive and the Present Perfect are a natural pair: both care about the result rather than the doer. That is why this form is everywhere in news, reports and business writing.

The ban on naming a finished past time applies here too: was built in 1990, but has been built with no year attached.

  • A result that is visible now
  • News about something that has just happened
  • Reporting completed work
  • An unfinished period: today, this week
Active → passive
  • activeSomeone has taken my umbrella.
    passiveMy umbrella has been taken.
    Someone simply vanishes — that is the whole point.
  • passiveThe report has already been sent.
    already goes between has and been.
  • passiveNothing has been decided yet.
    yet goes at the end.
The Native Mental Trigger

The receiver is the topic; the action is finished and its result stands now. When it happened doesn't matter — only that it's done and the effect is here.

The thought: “It's been done — the result is in front of me now, no time stated.”
When a native reaches for it — each with an example
  • Finished action, present resultThe problem has been solved.
  • Experience to now, no dateThis book has been translated into 30 languages.
  • Just completedYour order has just been shipped.
Signal words — each in a sentence
  • alreadyThe email has already been sent.
  • yetHas the form been signed yet?
  • justThe floor has just been washed.
  • so farTen tickets have been sold so far.
Past · 04

Past Simple Passive

it was done then
+it was done · they were done
−wasn't / weren't done
?Was it done? · Were they done?
What it means

The main passive form for telling a story: history, biography, a description of events. This is also where by shows up most often — when the doer does matter after all: painted by Turner.

Watch the number: was for singular, were for plural. A slip here is audible immediately.

  • A finished action on the object in the past
  • Historical facts and dates
  • Authorship: written, filmed, built by someone
Active → passive
  • activeSomeone stole my bike last night.
    passiveMy bike was stolen last night.
    The doer is unknown — exactly the case the passive exists for.
  • passiveThis church was built in 1785.
    There is a date, so it is was built, not has been built.
  • passiveThe letters were written by his mother.
    Here the doer matters and stays, behind by.
  • passiveWere you invited to the party?
    The person invited becomes the subject.
The Native Mental Trigger

The receiver is the topic; the action happened and finished at a named past time; the doer is unknown, obvious, or unimportant. The default passive of stories and history.

The thought: “It was done, then, and it's over — receiver first.”
When a native reaches for it — each with an example
  • Finished action at a past timeThe bridge was built in 1890.
  • Doer unknownMy bike was stolen last night.
  • Historical factsAmerica was discovered in 1492.
Signal words — each in a sentence
  • yesterdayThe letter was sent yesterday.
  • agoIt was decided a week ago.
  • in 1890 / last weekThe law was passed in 2019.
Past · 05

Past Continuous Passive

it was being done at that moment
+it was being done
−wasn't / weren't being done
?Was it being done?
What it means

Rare but alive. It needs a second coordinate — an exact time or another event: "while this was being done, that happened".

The chain was being done is the longest the past passive will carry. Beyond it the language gives up: there is no had been being done.

  • A process on the object at a specific past moment
  • Background for a short event
Active → passive
  • activeThey were painting the house when I arrived.
    passiveThe house was being painted when I arrived.
    The bar was running and the dot "I arrived" landed inside it.
  • passiveThe car was being repaired all day yesterday.
    all day sets the length of the bar.
The Native Mental Trigger

The receiver is the focus; the action was in progress on it at a past moment, often as the background that a shorter event interrupts.

The thought: “It was being done to it, then, mid-action.”
When a native reaches for it — each with an example
  • In progress at a past momentAt noon the office was being cleaned.
  • Interrupted backgroundThe car was being repaired when I called.
Signal words — each in a sentence
  • while / asWhile the house was being painted, we moved out.
  • whenThe bridge was being tested when it failed.
  • at 8 last nightAt 8 last night dinner was still being cooked.
Past · 06

Past Perfect Passive

it had been done before that moment
+it had been done
−hadn't been done
?Had it been done?
What it means

Needed in exactly the same situations as the active Past Perfect: to show that one thing happened before another. The second point is compulsory — without it the form is not used.

A frequent visitor to reported speech: He said the room had been booked.

  • An action on the object earlier than another past event
  • Reported speech
  • Explaining the cause of a past situation
Active → passive
  • activeSomeone had cleaned the room before we came.
    passiveThe room had been cleaned before we came.
    The cleaning came before the arrival.
  • passiveThe money had been spent long before that.
    The arc reaches a past point, not "now".
The Native Mental Trigger

The receiver is the topic; the action had already been completed before another past moment — the passive of 'before the past', and a common way to give the cause of a past state.

The thought: “It had already been done before that past point.”
When a native reaches for it — each with an example
  • Completed before another past actionBy the time we arrived, the meal had been cooked.
  • The cause of a past stateThe floor was wet; it had just been mopped.
Signal words — each in a sentence
  • before / afterThe room had been booked before we asked.
  • by the timeBy the time he came, the decision had been made.
  • alreadyIt had already been sold.
Future · 07

Future Simple Passive

it will be done
+it will be done
−won't be done
?Will it be done?
What it means

Its home ground is notices, service promises and contracts: You will be contacted shortly. The passive sounds official here and removes the question of who will act.

Remember the conditional and time clauses: after if, when, as soon as the future is not used — it is is done, not will be done.

  • A future action on the object
  • Notices, instructions, official promises
  • Predictions and plans
Active → passive
  • activeThey will announce the results tomorrow.
    passiveThe results will be announced tomorrow.
    will be + V3 — there is no being anywhere here.
  • passiveYou will be contacted within 24 hours.
    The person becomes the subject — very common in service language.
  • passiveThe order will be sent when payment is received.
    Present tense after when, though the meaning is future.
The Native Mental Trigger

The receiver is the topic; the action will be done to it in the future; the doer is unknown or irrelevant, or the sentence is a promise or a schedule.

The thought: “It will be done — receiver first, doer set aside.”
When a native reaches for it — each with an example
  • Future action on the objectThe results will be announced tomorrow.
  • Promise / reassuranceYou will be met at the airport.
  • Predicted or scheduledThe new road will be opened next year.
Signal words — each in a sentence
  • tomorrow / next weekThe winners will be notified next week.
  • soonIt will be fixed soon.
  • in 2030The line will be extended in 2030.
Future · 08

Future Perfect Passive

it will have been done by the deadline
+it will have been done
−won't have been done
?Will it have been done?
What it means

It lives exclusively next to the word by: by Friday, by the time you arrive. That is what plants the flag the readiness is measured against.

You meet it in contracts, plans and project documents — wherever the deadline matters and the contractor does not.

  • Work completed by a future deadline
  • Contractual obligations
Active → passive
  • activeThey will have finished the bridge by June.
    passiveThe bridge will have been finished by June.
    by June is the flag on the axis.
  • passiveEverything will have been prepared by the time you arrive.
    The flag is set by an event rather than a date.
The Native Mental Trigger

The receiver is the topic; standing at a future deadline and looking back, the action will be finished on it by then.

The thought: “By that future point it'll have been done.”
When a native reaches for it — each with an example
  • Finished before a future pointBy Friday the report will have been submitted.
Signal words — each in a sentence
  • by 2030 / by thenBy then the bridge will have been completed.
  • by the timeBy the time you read this, it will have been fixed.

What does not exist

Four forms assemble correctly by the rule but are absent from the language. The reason is not grammar but sound: they put two forms of be back to back, which is heavy enough that speakers always rebuild the sentence instead.

Non-existent formWhat people say instead
will be being doneFuture Simple Passive: The road will be repaired at that time.
has been being donePresent Perfect Passive, or switch to the active: They have been repairing the road for a week.
had been being donePast Perfect Passive: The road had been repaired by then.
will have been being doneActive only: They will have been repairing the road for a year.

The practical takeaway. If the meaning really calls for duration in a perfect, just put the sentence back into the active. That is not a defeat or a simplification — it is what native speakers do. The passive is good where the doer is unwanted; the moment you have to name one anyway, the active is shorter and clearer.

A second group of verbs never goes passive at all, even in simple tenses — the intransitive ones and the verbs of state: happen, occur, arrive, come, go, sleep, die, appear, belong, lack, resemble, suit, fit, have (in the sense of "own"). They have no object to promote to subject.

A common mistake

The accident was happened. → The accident happened.

He was died last year. → He died last year.

This book is belonged to me. → This book belongs to me.

One logic covers all three: these verbs do not do anything to anybody, so there is nothing to turn around.

Part 425:00

The passive after modals and in infinitives

The formula does not change — only what stands in front of be does. After any modal verb comes the bare form, so you simply get modal + be + V3.

StructureExample
must / should / can be doneThe form must be signed.
can't be doneIt can't be done in a day.
may / might be doneThe flight might be delayed.
have to be doneThis has to be fixed today.
should have been doneIt should have been done yesterday — and it wasn't.
to be done — infinitiveI want to be paid on time.
to have been done — perfect infinitiveHe seems to have been forgotten.
being done — gerundShe hates being interrupted.
having been doneHaving been warned, he left.

Look at the last three rows. These are the structures that let English compress a whole subordinate clause into one form: not "she hates it when people interrupt her" but she hates being interrupted. Learners tend to build the long version; the short one is what sounds native.

Part 529:40

Special cases

Five situations where the passive behaves differently from the basic pattern.

Two objects give two passives

Verbs like give, tell, send, show, offer, ask, teach, pay, promise take two objects: to whom, and what. Either can become the subject — and English almost always picks the person.

by or with

by — who did it: The window was broken by a boy.

with — what it was done with: The window was broken with a stone.

They coexist happily in one sentence: The window was broken by a boy with a stone. Mixing them up is expensive — broken with a boy says the boy was used as the tool.

Phrasal verbs keep their preposition

If the verb is made of two words, the preposition stays in place, right after the V3, even when that leaves it stranded at the end of the sentence with nothing after it. It looks odd but it is standard.

Somebody looked after the children. → The children were looked after.

They sent for the doctor. → The doctor was sent for.

Nobody has slept in this bed. → This bed hasn't been slept in.

get instead of be — the spoken passive

In informal speech get often replaces be. The flavour is suddenness, an event rather than a state, and often something unwelcome: He got fired. They got married. My phone got stolen.

In business writing and official texts the get-passive is not used — only be.

"It is said that…" — impersonal structures

When the doer is "people in general", English has two passive structures, and both are worth knowing:

It is said that he lives in Paris.

He is said to live in Paris. — the same thing, shorter and more literary.

The same works with believe, think, know, report, expect, consider: The company is expected to grow.

Part 636:00

Common mistakes

The eight places where passive sentences actually go wrong. If you remember only this part of the page, your error rate will drop noticeably.

A passive built from an intransitive verb
If the verb takes no object, it has no passive at all. was happened · was appeared · was arrived · was died All impossible. Use plain happened, appeared, arrived, died.
Missing "being" in the present continuous
The road is repaired now actually means "the road is in a repaired state". The road is being repaired now.
Second form instead of third
The passive always takes V3, never V2. With regular verbs they look identical and the error hides; with irregular ones it shows. was wrote · was took · was gave → was written · was taken · was given
do / does / did in a passive question
The passive already has its own auxiliary; a second one is not needed. Did the letter was sent? → Was the letter sent?
be agreeing with the wrong word
be agrees with the new subject, not with the original doer. The documents were signed. — not was signed, even though one person signed them.
State confused with process
Two different sentences, both correct, with different meanings. The house is built. — it is a built house, that is its state. The house is being built. — construction is under way.
Dropping "to" after a passive causative
Active make takes a bare infinitive, but the passive restores to. He was made go. → He was made to go.
Forgetting that some things are always passive
A few expressions only exist in the passive. I was born in 1998. — never I borned. Also be supposed to, be located.

When the passive helps and when it hurts

The passive is a tool, not a decoration. In some school traditions it is treated as a mark of grown-up language; in English the opposite is true — too much passive is considered a stylistic weakness.

The passive is right when the doer is unknown, unimportant or obvious; in scientific and technical writing where the process matters more than the author; in news and official documents; and when the object is the main character of the paragraph and you do not want to switch away from it.

The passive gets in the way when the doer is known and matters. Compare: The decision was made to close the office — made by whom is unclear, and the sentence sounds evasive. The board decided to close the office — short and clear.

A practical rule. In conversation the passive is a small fraction of what people say. If three passives appear in a row in your paragraph, two of them are almost certainly better in the active. English likes an action to have a doer standing at the front.

The Native Mental Trigger

Before speaking, the native asks one question: who or what belongs at the FRONT of this sentence — the doer, or the thing it's done to? And do I even know, or care, who did it? If the doer is the natural topic, they stay active. If the receiver is the topic — or the doer is unknown, obvious, or better left unnamed — they flip to passive and let the doer drop, or trail behind by.

The thought: “The important thing here isn't who did it — it's the thing it happened to. Put that first.”
The difference, from the inside
Active — the doer leads
Someone stole my car.
“The doer goes first — but here it's a vague someone, a weak thing to open with.”
Passive — the receiver leads
My car was stolen.
“My car is what I'm talking about; the thief is unknown and irrelevant, so I drop them entirely.”
Why a native flips to passive — each with an example
  • The doer is unknownMy bike was stolen last night.
  • The doer is obvious (police, people in general)He was arrested at the airport.
  • The doer simply doesn't matterThe bridge was built in 1890.
  • Keep the spotlight on the topicThe patient was examined and then sent home.
  • Tactfully avoid naming who's at faultMistakes were made.
  • Science / process, not the authorThe sample was heated to 90°C.
  • The doer IS the news → send it to the end with byHamlet was written by Shakespeare.
Hard sentences — and the thought behind them
  • The proposal is being reviewed and will be voted on next week.
    Native thought: Two passives keep the spotlight on the proposal the whole way through; the reviewers and voters are beside the point.
  • It is widely believed that the deal will collapse.
    Native thought: I don't want to name who believes it — the impersonal passive lets me report a general opinion with no source attached.
  • You'll be met at the airport and taken to the hotel.
    Native thought: The listener (you) is the topic; who meets and drives them is unimportant — this is reassurance, not detail.
  • The window must have been left open.
    Native thought: Passive stacked on a modal — I focus on the window's state; who left it open I don't know, hence must have been.
Part 846:00

Cheat sheet: building a passive in five steps

1. Find the object of the active sentence
The thing that answers "what?" or "whom?". No object means no passive.
2. Move it to the front
It is now the subject, and be will agree with it.
3. Identify the tense of the active verb
That is the tense be goes into — the main verb stops showing tense entirely.
4. Put the main verb into the third form
V3 and only V3, and it never changes again.
5. Decide what happens to the doer
Important — send it to the end behind by. Unimportant, unknown or obvious — drop it entirely. That is normal, not a loss of meaning.
The listening test
Read it aloud. If you hear two forms of be together (be being, been being), that form does not exist — rebuild the sentence or return it to the active.

And the main point. The passive adds no new tense to a sentence — its timeline is identical to the active one. Only the point of view changes: instead of the one who acts, the one acted upon steps forward.
