Prepositions cannot really be translated — they have to be seen. Nearly every one draws a relationship in space and then carries that same relationship over to time and to abstract things. Reading the diagrams

Every diagram on this page is built from the same few elements. The coloured shape is the thing being talked about; the grey shapes are the landmark it is positioned against.

The coloured dot
The object or person whose position we are describing. Its colour shows which group the preposition belongs to.
The grey shapes
The landmark: a box, a surface, a wall, a road. The landmark itself does not matter — the relationship to it does.
The arrow
Movement. If a preposition answers "where to?", its diagram will always have an arrow.
The axis with an arrowhead
A timeline: past on the left, future on the right, red dashes for "now". The same axis used in the tenses guide.

The central idea of this page. A preposition is not a word with a fixed translation — it is a picture. "At university" and "in the box" use different English prepositions where many languages would use one and the same word, because the two situations look different: a point of activity versus an enclosed volume. Once you hold the picture in mind instead of a translation, the choice becomes close to automatic.

Point → line → volume

The three central prepositions of English — at, on, in — form a scale of precision. The same principle governs both place and time. Learn this scale and half of all prepositions fall into place.

at
A point. A place with no size, a moment with no length.
at the door
at the bus stop
at 6 o'clock
on
A surface or a line. There is contact, but no volume.
on the table
on the wall
on Monday
in
A volume. Enclosed on all sides — by walls, by borders, by time.
in the box
in London
in May

The scale works in time too. at 6 o'clock is a point on the axis. on Monday is a day, a cell of the calendar. in May is a large stretch that we are inside of. The bigger the piece of time, the more "voluminous" the preposition.

Addresses follow the same logic. at 25 Baker Street is an exact point. on Baker Street is a street, a line. in London is a city, a volume. One dimension up each step.

One important break in the logic. With vehicles the deciding factor is not size but posture: on the bus / train / plane (you can stand and walk — it is a platform), but in the car / taxi (you sit inside a tight box). The same explains on a bike and on a horse — you are on top.

One question settles the choice

Ask yourself: is this a point, a surface, or a volume?

"He is waiting at the bus stop" — a stop is a point on the map → at the bus stop.

"A photo on the wall" — a wall is a surface → on the wall.

"Keys in the bag" — a bag is a volume → in the bag.

The Native Mental Trigger

Before a native picks a preposition of place or time, they don't recall a rule — they see a shape. Is this a point, a surface, or a volume? A point with no size → at. A surface or line you touch → on. A space you're enclosed inside → in. The one twist they carry: with vehicles it's posture, not size — a bus is a platform you stand on, a car is a box you sit in.

The thought: “Point, surface, or volume? Once I see the shape, the word is chosen.”
One place, three shapes — “the corner”
at — a point
I'll wait at the corner.
“Where two streets meet — a dot on the map.”
on — a surface / spot
The shop is on the corner.
“It sits on that outer angle of the block.”
in — an enclosed angle
The lamp is in the corner.
“The inside angle of the room, walls on two sides.”
Same shape-logic, classic confusions — each with an example
  • on the bus / train (a platform you stand on)I'm on the train — I'll call you at the station.
  • in the car / taxi (a tight box you sit in)She's in the car, waiting outside.
  • on time = at the exact scheduled momentThe train left on time, to the minute.
  • in time = with a margin, not lateWe got there just in time to catch it.
  • at the end = a point (place or time)Turn left at the end of the street.
  • in the end = finally, after allIn the end, we decided to stay.
  • at school = the institution / activityThe kids are at school until three.
  • in the school = inside the buildingThe meeting is in the school, room 4.
Hard sentences — and the thought behind them
  • I'll meet you at the station at six, on platform 4.
    Native thought: The station is a point on the map (at), six is a point in time (at), and a platform is a long flat surface (on).
  • There's a crack in the wall and a poster on the wall.
    Native thought: The crack is inside the material — a volume (in); the poster sits on the flat face — a surface (on).
  • We're on the train now, arriving in ten minutes.
    Native thought: A train is a platform I stand on (posture, on); ten minutes is a stretch I'm counting inside (in).
  • She's in hospital in Leeds, on the third floor.
    Native thought: A patient's state and a city are both volumes (in); a floor is a flat level you stand on (on).

Prepositions of time

The diagrams use the same axis as the tenses guide: past on the left, future on the right, red dashes for the moment of speaking. Each preposition shows which part of the axis it occupies: a point, a stretch, a boundary or a distance.

ata point with no length
The image

A pinprick on the axis. A moment has no extent: we are not inside seven o'clock, we simply point at a mark on the scale.

Everything else follows. Clock times are the smallest divisions, so they take at. But larger periods land here too if the language treats them as one undivided block rather than a set of days: night, the weekend, Christmas as a season.

The test: if you can "live inside" the period day after day, it is in. If it collapses into a single notch, it is at.

All meanings
  1. Clock timeat 7 o'clock · at 6:15 · at noon · at midnightThe core use — divisions of the clock.
  2. Parts of the day treated as a pointat night · at dawn · at sunrise · at sunsetNight is the odd one out of in the morning / afternoon / evening. Historically it was felt as one unbroken stretch of darkness with no internal structure.
  3. Festive periodsat Christmas · at Easter · at the weekendThe whole season. A single day inside it takes on: on Christmas Day. In American English the weekend is on the weekend.
  4. Ageat the age of 20 · at 40 he changed careersA point on the scale of a life.
  5. The edge of a periodat the beginning of the year · at the end of the monthAn edge is a point — note at, not in.
  6. A moment relative to anotherat the same time · at that moment · at first · at last · at onceAll of these mark a notch, not a stretch.
Where at is not used
  • Days of the week and dates — on Why: a day has length; morning, afternoon and evening fit inside it. It is a cell of the calendar that an event is laid onto, not a notch on the clock — and at can only mark a notch. The meeting is at Monday. The meeting is on Monday.
  • Months, years, centuries, seasons — in Why: life goes on inside a month or a year — it is a container you are within. A point cannot hold anything. She was born at May 1998. She was born in May 1998.
  • Morning, afternoon, evening — in Why: these are stretches with an inside. Only night falls out of the pattern. I usually read at the evening. I usually read in the evening.
  • After last, next, this, every — no preposition at all Why: these words are themselves time markers and turn the whole phrase into an adverbial. An adverb needs no preposition, exactly as yesterday and tomorrow need none. We met at last Monday. We met last Monday.
Boundaries
  • at night vs in the night — the first is "at night, in general"; the second exists but means "during that particular night" and sounds literary.
  • at the end of vs in the end — "at the edge of something specific" vs "in the end, finally".
ona day as a calendar cell
The image

An event laid on the surface of a day. A calendar is a grid of cells; a day is one cell, and you place something on it. Hence a surface preposition where the meaning feels like "in".

The rule barely has exceptions: if the phrase contains a day name or a date, it is on. And a named day pulls the preposition onto itself: in the morning, but on Monday morning.

All meanings
  1. Days of the weekon Monday · on Fridays · on weekdaysThe plural adds regularity: "every Friday".
  2. Dateson 5 May · on May 5th · on 12 March 1998Any calendar date.
  3. Special dayson my birthday · on Christmas Day · on New Year's EveThe day itself, not the season.
  4. Day + part of the dayon Monday morning · on the evening of 5 MayThe named day outranks the part of the day and takes the preposition.
  5. The moment an event happenson arrival · on his return · on hearing the newson + noun or -ing means "as soon as": On arriving, he called us.
  6. Punctualityon time · on scheduleThe event landed exactly on its slot in the timetable.
Where on is not used
  • Parts of the day with no day named — in Why: a morning on its own is a stretch, not a calendar cell. It becomes a cell only when a specific day is attached to it. I'll call you on the morning. I'll call you in the morning. · I'll call you on Monday morning.
  • Months and years — in Why: a month contains days. You cannot lay an event on top of it, only step inside it. He arrived on May. He arrived in May.
  • After last, next, this, every — no preposition Why: the determiner has already anchored the time to the moment of speaking and the phrase has become adverbial. A second marker is redundant. See you on next Friday. See you next Friday.
  • With tomorrow, yesterday, today — no preposition Why: these are adverbs, not nouns. A preposition needs a noun after it, so it cannot stand in front of an adverb. Let's meet on tomorrow. Let's meet tomorrow.
Boundaries
  • on time vs in time — exactly on schedule vs early enough, not too late. The train arrived on time (to the minute); we got there in time (with something to spare).
  • on Christmas Day vs at Christmas — the specific day vs the whole festive period.
ina volume of time — and "from now"
The image

A stretch that we are inside of. A month holds days, a year holds months — it is a container, not a notch. Plenty happens within it.

in has a second, quite different job: "this much time from now" in the future. Formally it is the same image — you measure out a container of time and come out the other end — but it is easily confused with after, within and through, so it gets its own card below.

All meanings
  1. Months, years, centuriesin May · in 2026 · in the 1990s · in the 19th centuryAll large calendar stretches.
  2. Seasonsin winter · in spring · in the summerThe article is optional.
  3. Parts of the dayin the morning · in the afternoon · in the eveningExcept night, which takes at.
  4. Life stages and erasin my childhood · in his twenties · in the past · in the futureA container that a life unfolds inside.
  5. This much time from nowin two hours · in a week · in a momentThe mirror of ago: two hours ago ↔ in two hours.
  6. How long something tookHe wrote it in two days.The work fitted inside that span.
  7. Early enoughin time · just in timeWe got inside the acceptable window.
Where in is not used
  • Days and dates — on Why: you do not go inside a day, you lay things on it. It is a surface-cell, not a volume. The exam is in Monday. The exam is on Monday.
  • Clock times — at Why: a moment has no interior; nothing fits inside seven o'clock. It is a notch, not a container. The film starts in 7 o'clock. The film starts at 7 o'clock.
  • Night — at Why: the one part of the day the language treats as a single undivided block. In the night exists but means "during that particular night" and sounds bookish. I can't sleep in the night. I can't sleep at night.
  • After last, next, this, every — no preposition Why: the phrase is already adverbial and needs no preposition. We moved here in last year. We moved here last year.
  • The edge of a period — at Why: the edge of a stretch is a boundary, and a boundary is a point. You are in the month but at its edge. I'll be free in the end of May. I'll be free at the end of May.
Boundaries
  • in two hours vs within two hours — exactly two hours from now vs at some point inside those two hours, no later.
  • in two hours vs for two hours — when vs how long.
  • in two days vs after two days — in counts from now; after counts from a named event.
fora measured length
The image

A stretch whose length has been measured. Where it sits on the axis does not matter — only "how much". After for comes an amount of time, never a date and never an event.

When the stretch reaches "now", English requires a perfect: I have lived here for ten years. A present simple here would describe a property, not a stretch.

All meanings
  1. Duration of an actionfor two hours · for a week · for ten yearsThe core use.
  2. Duration reaching the presentI've known him for ten years.The stretch runs into "now", so the verb goes perfect.
  3. A planned spanWe're staying for a week.A stretch in the future.
  4. Vague lengthfor a long time · for ages · for a while · for everApproximate, but still a length.
Where for is not used
  • Before a named event — during Why: after for comes an answer to "how much", i.e. a number. A meeting or a war is not a number — it answers "when". He fell asleep for the film. He fell asleep during the film.
  • Before a starting point — since Why: Monday is a moment, not a length. for measures the stretch, since marks its left edge. She has been ill for Friday. She has been ill since Friday.
  • With all — no preposition Why: all day is already an adverbial of time and works on its own, like yesterday. It rained for all day. It rained all day.
Boundaries
  • for vs since — how much vs starting when. for three days ↔ since Monday can describe the very same stretch from two sides.
  • for vs in — He wrote it for two days (spent two days writing, maybe unfinished) vs in two days (finished within two days).
sincea start point that reaches now
The image

The left edge of a stretch whose right edge is "now". That second part is essential: since does not merely mark a beginning, it implies that the stretch runs all the way to the moment of speaking.

That is why it is welded to the perfect. If the action had finished in the past, the stretch would not reach "now" and you would need from instead.

All meanings
  1. A starting momentsince Monday · since 2019 · since morningAlways a point, never a length.
  2. An event as the startsince I was a child · since we met · since thenHere since works as a conjunction with a full clause after it.
  3. Emphaticever since"From that very moment right up to today".
  4. Cause, not timeSince you're here, let's start.A separate meaning: "given that". A softer synonym of because.
Where since is not used
  • Before a length — for Why: "two hours" is a measured length and since can only point at a moment. Ask yourself whether this answers "how much" or "starting when". I have been waiting since two hours. I have been waiting for two hours. · I have been waiting since two o'clock.
  • When the far end is also named — from ... to Why: since implies the stretch runs to "now" and has no stated right edge. Once you name the end, you need the from … to pair. I work since Monday to Friday. I work from Monday to Friday.
  • With the present simple Why: a stretch that starts in the past and reaches the present forces a perfect. The present simple describes a property, and a property has no starting point. I work here since 2019. I have worked here since 2019.
duringinside a named event
The image

We are inside an event that has a name. The word comes from a verb meaning "to last": while the meeting lasts, something happens inside it.

Hence the strict requirement for what follows: a noun naming an event or period — a meeting, a war, the night, the holidays. A number cannot go there.

All meanings
  1. Inside an eventduring the meeting · during the film · during the warThe action happened somewhere inside — not necessarily throughout.
  2. Inside a periodduring the night · during the holidays · during the summerThe same, with a calendar period.
  3. ThroughoutIt rained during the whole night.With whole the meaning shifts to "the entire time".
Where during is not used
  • Before a number — for Why: during answers "when exactly", by naming an event. A number answers "how much", which is for's job. We talked during three hours. We talked for three hours.
  • Before a full clause — the conjunction while Why: during is a preposition, and a preposition by definition takes a noun. As soon as there is a subject and a verb, you need a conjunction. During I was in London, I visited the museum. While I was in London, I visited the museum.
Boundaries
  • during vs while — preposition vs conjunction. during the lesson ↔ while we were studying: same meaning, different grammar.
  • during vs throughout — somewhere inside vs from edge to edge.
bya deadline you must not cross
The image

A flag marking a boundary. The action may happen anywhere to the left of it — a week early is fine — but not later.

That is why by pulls perfect tenses after it: at the flag the result must already be there. will have finished by June.

All meanings
  1. A deadlineby Friday · by 6 p.m. · by the end of the monthThe core use.
  2. A deadline set by an eventby the time you come · by thenNo future tense after by the time.
  3. By the present momentby nowThe boundary is "now".
Where by is not used
  • With durative verbs — until Why: by marks a boundary for a one-off action: finish, send, arrive. Verbs like wait, work and sleep fill the whole stretch, and they need until. Wait by six o'clock. Wait until six o'clock.
  • After by the time — no future tense Why: by the time introduces a time clause, and English does not put will in those — the same rule as after when, if and as soon as. By the time you will come, I'll be gone. By the time you come, I'll be gone.
Boundaries
  • by vs until — get it done by this moment (one action, a point) vs keep going until this moment (a process, a stretch). Test on the verb: one-off → by; durative → until.
until · tilla process running up to a boundary
The image

The whole stretch up to the flag, not the flag itself. Something lasts and stops at that moment. On the diagram it is the stretch that is filled in.

till is the same word, more colloquial. Historically till is the older form and until is till with an intensifying prefix.

All meanings
  1. The end of a lasting actionuntil Friday · till nine · open till lateThe core use.
  2. As a conjunctionWait until I call you.A full clause follows — with a present tense for future meaning.
  3. The not ... until patternHe didn't arrive until midnight.Means "only then, not before".
  4. The end of a rangefrom Monday until FridayInterchangeable with to.
Where until is not used
  • With a one-off action at a deadline — by Why: until fills the whole stretch, so it needs a verb that can last. "Finish" happens instantaneously and cannot be stretched — it needs the flag, by. I must finish the report until six. I must finish the report by six.
  • In space — to Why: until lives on the time axis only. For distance use the ordinary vector to, or the phrase as far as. Walk until the corner and turn left. Walk to the corner and turn left.
  • No future tense after it Why: it is a conjunction of time, and after those English uses the present even for future meaning. Wait until I will call you. Wait until I call you.
from ... toa stretch with both ends marked
The image

A stretch with both edges named. It is the same pair that works in space (from London to Paris) — time is just another scale.

from ... until is equally correct and puts slightly more weight on the closing boundary.

All meanings
  1. A stretch of timefrom Monday to Friday · from 9 to 5 · from 1990 until 2005The core use.
  2. Complete coveragefrom beginning to end · from start to finishFixed phrases, no articles.
  3. Repetitionfrom time to time · from day to dayMoving from one point to the next, over and over.
Where this pair does not work
  • When the end is not named — since Why: from … to draws a stretch with two edges. When the right edge is "now" and goes unspoken, you need since plus a perfect. I have lived here from 2019. I have lived here since 2019.
  • Do not blend it with between Why: two different structures: from pairs with to, between pairs with and. Mixing them produces a hybrid that does not exist. Come between 2 to 4. Come between 2 and 4. · Come from 2 to 4.
beforeeverything to the left
The image

The whole part of the axis to the left of a point. The word literally means "in front" (be + fore) — and on a timeline "in front" means earlier: the past comes first.

It works both as a preposition and as a conjunction, which is what sets it apart from during (which has to hand over to while).

All meanings
  1. Earlier than a momentbefore lunch · before 2020 · before the warPreposition + noun.
  2. Earlier than an actionbefore you leave · before I forgetConjunction + clause. No future tense after it.
  3. Previously, in generalI've never seen it before. · the day before yesterdayAs an adverb.
  4. Soonbefore longA fixed phrase meaning "shortly".
Where before is not used
  • "In front of" in space — in front of Why: modern English has assigned before to the time axis. Its spatial sense survives only in formal and literary registers and sounds odd in ordinary speech. He was standing before the house. He was standing in front of the house.
  • No future tense after it Why: the conjunction already shows the action lies ahead, so a second future marker is redundant. Call me before you will leave. Call me before you leave.
aftereverything to the right
The image

The whole part of the axis to the right of a point — the mirror of before. Also both preposition and conjunction.

Do not confuse it with in: after counts from a named event, while in counts from the moment of speaking. "Back in two hours" is in, because the reference point is now.

All meanings
  1. Later than a momentafter the meeting · after lunch · after 2020Preposition + noun.
  2. Later than an actionafter I finish · after she had leftConjunction + clause.
  3. Following a periodafter two hours of waiting · after a whileCounted from an implied past event.
  4. Behind, in pursuitrun after him · look after the childrenThe spatial sense "behind" — which is where look after comes from.
  5. One after anotherday after day · one after anotherRepetition in a chain.
Where after is not used
  • For the future measured from now — in Why: after always counts from a named event: after the meeting, after lunch. When the reference point is the moment of speaking, you need in. I'll be back after two hours. I'll be back in two hours. · After two hours of waiting, he left.
  • No future tense after it Why: the same time-clause rule as with before, when and until. I'll call you after I will finish. I'll call you after I finish.
agodistance to the left of now
The image

A measured distance backwards from the moment of speaking. Formally it is not a preposition but an adverb — which is why it is the only item here that goes after the amount of time rather than before it.

The reference point is always "now", and the event lies wholly in the past. Hence the iron consequence: ago requires Past Simple and is incompatible with the perfect, which refuses any named past time.

All meanings
  1. This much time backtwo days ago · five minutes ago · a year agoIts only meaning — but a frequent one.
  2. Vaguely long agolong ago · ages ago · not long agoThe distance is approximate.
Where ago is not used
  • Not before the amount of time, but after it Why: ago is an adverb, not a preposition. A preposition stands in front of the word it governs; an adverb follows it. He left ago two hours. He left two hours ago.
  • Not with the perfect Why: ago names a specific moment in the past, and the Present Perfect cannot take one — it talks about the state now, not about a point on the axis. He has left two days ago. He left two days ago.
  • Not replaced by before Why: they have different reference points. ago counts from "now"; before counts from an event in the past. I saw him before two days. I saw him two days ago. · I had seen him two days before the accident.
Boundaries
  • ago vs before — from now vs from a past point.
  • ago vs in — exact mirrors: two hours ago ↔ in two hours.
indistance to the right of now
The image

The mirror of ago. The same measured length, laid out on the other side of the moment of speaking. Hold the pair in mind as a unit: two hours ago ↔ in two hours.

Three wrong candidates compete for this slot — through, after and within — and each is wrong for a different reason. The boundaries below sort them out.

All meanings
  1. This much time from nowin two hours · in a week · in ten minutesThe reference point is always the moment of speaking.
  2. Very soonin a moment · in no time · in a secondFixed phrases.
Boundaries
  • in two hours vs within two hours — exactly two hours from now vs no later than that.
  • in two hours vs after two hours — from now vs from a past event.
  • in vs through — through means physically passing through a volume and never transfers to the time axis in this sense.
withinsomewhere inside the limit
The image

A point at an unknown place inside a stretch. We know the boundary we will not cross, but not where exactly inside the event will land.

The difference from in is subtle but real: in two hours promises exactly two hours, within two hours promises "not later".

betweena stretch with two named edges
The image

A gap bounded by two named moments. Both edges are compulsory — and they are joined by the conjunction and, never by the preposition to.

All meanings
  1. A span of timebetween 2 and 4 o'clock · between Monday and FridayThe core use.
  2. In the gap between eventsbetween meetings · between classesThe gap is defined by events rather than clock times.
  3. A historical erabetween the warsA period squeezed between two landmarks.

over · throughoutacross the whole period
The image

An arc covering the period end to end. It is the same over that means "above" in space: the action arches over the whole stretch of time.

All meanings
  1. A total across a periodover the past year · over the last decadeOften with a perfect: Sales have grown over the past year.

past · totelling the time
The image

A clock face is the same axis, bent into a circle. In the first half of the hour the hand has already gone past the mark, so we count what has elapsed: past. In the second half it is still travelling towards the next mark, so we count what remains: to.

These are exactly the same past ("by and beyond") and to ("towards a goal") as in space. Nothing new to learn — you only have to remember that English names the hour just gone, so half past six is 6:30.

All meanings
  1. First half of the hour — pastten past six (6:10) · quarter past six (6:15) · half past six (6:30)Minutes elapsed since the named hour.
  2. Second half of the hour — totwenty to seven (6:40) · quarter to seven (6:45) · five to nine (8:55)Minutes remaining until the next hour.
  3. On the hoursix o'clock · at six sharpNo preposition at all.
  4. The digital waysix fifteen · six forty-fiveIncreasingly common, especially in the US.
Watch out
  • half seven means 7:30, not 6:30 Why: in colloquial British English past simply drops out, but the hour named stays the one just gone — the shortened form keeps the full form's arithmetic. half seven = 6:30 half seven = 7:30 · half past seven = 7:30
  • Minutes beyond 30 are not counted with past Why: after the half hour the hand is closer to the next mark, so the language switches to counting what is left. It's forty past six. It's twenty to seven.
around · aboutroughly — a circle round a point
The image

A point blurred by a circle. Approximation is literally "around" the exact value — the same image as spatial around.

With about the preposition at is usually kept (at about six); with around it is usually dropped (around six). Both are correct.

All meanings

on time · in timetwo different kinds of "on time"
The image

The difference falls straight out of the two prepositions' core images.

on time — the event landed exactly on its slot in the schedule, to the minute. This is about punctuality.

in time — the event got inside the acceptable window, before it was too late. This is about having a margin, not about precision.

All meanings
  1. on time — exactly as scheduledThe train arrived on time. · Please be on time.Neither early nor late.
  2. in time — early enoughWe got there just in time. · in time to say goodbyeOften with just. Usually followed by an infinitive of purpose.
The test
  • If you could say "to the minute", it is on time. If you could say "we made it", it is in time.
at the end · in the endtwo different kinds of "at the end"

Prepositions of place

The most literal group: the diagram shows exactly where the thing is. This is also where English draws distinctions many languages do not — at / in / on the corner, on the tree against in the tree, at school against in the school.

The image

Prepositions of direction

These answer "where to?" — and every diagram grows an arrow. Many pairs differ by nothing else: in is where, into is where to; on is where, onto is where to.

toa vector that reaches its goal
The image

An arrow that lands on its target. In Old English tō meant "in the direction of, as far as". Two things live inside the word at once: a path and an endpoint that is reached. That is what separates to from towards, which has direction but no arrival, and from at, which has a point but no path.

All twelve meanings below are that one image carried over from physical space onto people, numbers, time and thought. Whenever you see "there is movement and the goal is reached", it is to — even if nobody is going anywhere.

All meanings

  2. The recipient — a human goalgive it to me · explain it to him · say it to her · write to meThe same vector, but the endpoint is a person. Many languages mark this with a case ending instead, so the preposition gets dropped.
  3. Which way something facesturn to the left · point to the door · with his back to the wallNo bodily movement, but the vector is there: something is oriented that way.
  4. The end of a rangefrom 9 to 5 · from Monday to Friday · count to ten · from A to ZA scale is a path too. from marks the start, to the point we reach.
  5. Attachment and belongingattached to the wall · belong to me · the key to the door · married to AnnaThe goal is reached and contact is made. A key fits "to" a lock, a person is bound "to" another — the endpoint of a connection.
  6. Answer, solution, reactionthe answer to my question · a solution to the problem · react to the newsAn answer is aimed at a question. That is why the noun answer takes to — while the verb answer takes no preposition at all.
  7. Comparison and ratiosuperior to · prefer tea to coffee · similar to · the score was 3 to 1We set one thing beside another and measure the distance from one to the other.

Where to is not used
  • home, there, here, abroad, upstairs, inside — no preposition Why: these are not nouns but adverbs of place, with the direction already built into them.   • arrive — in or at only Why: arrive pictures not the journey but the endpoint: the verb already says the path is over and you are there.   • enter — no preposition Why: the word comes from Latin intrare, "to go in". The "in" is already inside the root, and adding into says it twice. The idea of "into a club" or "at a meeting" suggests a preposition that English does not use here. She joined to the club last year. She joined the club last year. · We attended the meeting.
  • answer — verb without, noun with to Why: the verb answer is transitive: you answer a question the way you solve a problem. The noun answer is a thing directed at the question, and it needs the vector. Please answer to my question. Please answer my question. · I'm still waiting for an answer to my question.
  • call, phone, email — no preposition Why: the addressee is a direct object here: you call someone, not "to someone". Call to means shouting across a distance, which is not what you do on the telephone. Call to me when you arrive. Call me when you arrive.
  • tell — no preposition, but say — with to Why: the two verbs have different direct objects. tell means "to inform somebody", so the addressee is built into the meaning — it takes the direct-object slot, and the message follows second. With say the direct object is the words themselves; the slot is occupied, so the person has to be attached separately with the vector to. Tell to me what happened. · He said me the truth. Tell me what happened. · He said the truth to me. · He told me the truth.
  • marry — verb without, state with to Why: the verb marry is transitive — "take somebody as a spouse". But be married to describes an established connection, and English always fastens connections with to: attached to, the key to. He married to my sister in 2019. · She is married on a doctor. He married my sister in 2019. · She is married to a doctor.
  • discuss — no preposition Why: from Latin discutere, "to examine"; the topic examined is the direct object. Neither to nor about belongs here. We discussed about the budget for an hour. We discussed the budget for an hour.
  • After modal verbs — the bare infinitive Why: can, must, should and will were ordinary past-tense verbs in older English and always took an infinitive without to. That ancient habit has survived intact. I can to swim very well. I can swim very well.
  • After make, let, have — the bare infinitive Why: in these structures the action is felt as one unit with the command: not "made him so that he would go" but "made-him-go". A vector would break that unity. In the passive the unity dissolves and to comes back: He was made to go. She made him to apologize. She made him apologize. · He was made to apologize.
  • After would rather and had better — the bare infinitive Why: both phrases contain a comparative (rather, better) and behave more like modal verbs than ordinary ones. They inherited the pattern from them. I'd rather to stay at home. I'd rather stay at home. · You'd better go now.
  • help — to is optional Why: a rare case where both forms are correct. Without to the helping and the action merge into a single event; with to they feel slightly more separate. Speech usually drops it. Can you help me carry this? · Can you help me to carry this?
Boundaries
  • to vs towards — arrived vs merely headed that way. He walked to the door — he got there. He walked towards the door — he set off in its direction; what happened next is unstated.
  • to vs into — up to the object vs inside it. go to the house is approach the house; go into the house is step inside.
  • to vs at — a vector vs a target. throw the ball to me means for me to catch it; throw the ball at me means to hit me. The difference between a pass and an assault is one preposition.
  • to vs for — the recipient vs the beneficiary. give it to me puts it in my hands; buy it for me does it on my behalf.
  • to vs until — interchangeable in ranges (from 9 to 5), but a lone boundary in time needs until: wait until six, not wait to six.

Other relations

These prepositions cannot be drawn: they express not a position in space but a link between meanings — possession, cause, means, comparison. No diagram will help, but an image and a clear rule will.

ofa part growing out of a whole
The image

One thing grows out of another. Historically of is the same root as off and meant "away from": a part separated from a whole. Hence the roof of a house, a cup of tea, death of an illness.

English has a second way to show possession: the ending 's. The two have divided the labour, and knowing the split is what stops of from being overused.

The division: animate owners take 's (my brother's car); inanimate things take of (the roof of the house). Breaking it is not ungrammatical, just clumsy — and a native ear catches it at once.

All meanings
  1. Part of a wholethe roof of the house · the end of the story · the top of the pageThe main use with inanimate things.
  2. Quantity and measurea cup of tea · a piece of cake · a lot of people · a kilo of sugarA portion separated from a mass.
  3. Materiala table made of wood · a ring of goldThe material is still visible in the object. If it has changed beyond recognition, use from: wine is made from grapes.
  4. Possession (inanimate)the leg of the table · the name of the streetFor people, 's instead.
  5. Naming what exactlythe city of Rome · the problem of pollution · a period of two yearsThe second word spells out the first.
  6. Qualitya man of honour · a matter of time · of great importanceThe quality belongs to the thing.
  7. Causedie of cancer · afraid of dogs · tired of waitingThe state grew out of that source.
  8. One out of a setone of them · the best of all · most of the studentsA part extracted from a group. After a superlative it is nearly always of.
  9. A depiction of somethinga photo of my sister · a map of EuropeCareful: a photo of me shows me; a photo by me was taken by me.
  10. Datesthe 5th of MayThe British date format.
Where of is not used
  • Possession by people — use 's Why: English has two devices and has assigned them: a living being capable of owning takes 's, an inanimate object takes of. This is the car of my brother. This is my brother's car. · This is the roof of the house.
  • After despite Why: despite is a complete preposition and takes its noun directly. The of belongs to in spite of, where spite is a noun. Despite of the rain, we went out. Despite the rain, we went out. · In spite of the rain, we went out.
  • After discuss, enter, answer (as verbs) Why: all three are transitive — the object attaches directly, with no go-between. Let's discuss of the plan. Let's discuss the plan.
  • After behind, opposite, near Why: these are self-contained prepositions. The stray of creeps in by analogy with in front of, where it is genuinely required because front is a noun. The garden is behind of the house. The garden is behind the house.
  • With numerals and no determiner Why: of is needed only when you take a part out of a known set — and a set is known only if the, my or them marks it. With no determiner there is nothing to take a part from. I bought two of books. I bought two books. · I read two of the books you gave me.

because of · due to · owing tocause
The image

The event grew out of a cause. All three are prepositions, so a noun follows them, never a full clause.

All meanings
  1. Cause before a nounbecause of the rain · due to technical problemsThe core use.
  2. Cause before a clause — the conjunction becausebecause it was rainingHere the of disappears: this is a conjunction, not a preposition.
Where these do not work
  • Before a subject and a verb Why: because of is a preposition and can only govern a noun. A full clause attaches with a conjunction, and the of drops. We stayed home because of it was raining. We stayed home because it was raining. · We stayed home because of the rain.
  • due to at the start of a sentence Why: in strict usage due to modifies a noun and follows the verb be: The delay was due to fog. Sentence-initially because of is safer, though the distinction is fading in modern usage. Because of the fog, the flight was delayed. · The delay was due to fog.

  • Directly before a clause — although Why: a preposition cannot govern a whole clause. Either attach it through the fact that or switch to a conjunction. Despite he was tired, he kept working. Although he was tired, he kept working. · Despite the fact that he was tired, he kept working.

Common mistakes

Twelve places where preposition errors actually cluster. If you remember only this part of the page, your error rate drops noticeably.

depend on, not depend from
The image is not motion away from a source but resting on top of one — the verb literally means "hang from".It depends on the weather.
wait for, listen to
These verbs never take a bare object, however natural that feels.wait for the bus · listen to music
at university, not in the university
An institution as an activity is a point, not a volume.at university · at school · at work
arrive in / at, never arrive to
The verb pictures the endpoint, not the path — so it takes prepositions of place.arrive in Moscow · arrive at the airport
go home — no preposition
go to home is impossible: home is an adverb with the direction inside it.go home · come home · at home
in the photo, not on the photo
An image is a space with depth for English, not a flat surface.in the photo · in the picture · in the mirror
by car, but in my car
A mode of travel takes by with no article. A specific vehicle takes in or on.by car · in my car · on foot
married to
A connection is always fastened with to, like attached to.He is married to my sister.
half past six = 6:30
English names the hour just gone, not the one coming. Getting it backwards puts you out by a full hour.half past six = 6:30 · half seven = 7:30
in two hours, not through or after
Counting forward from the moment of speaking is always in.I'll call you in two hours.
despite, or in spite of — never despite of
Two structures that get welded into a non-existent third.despite the rain · in spite of the rain
explain it to me, but tell me
tell takes the person directly; explain and say need the vector to.Explain it to me. · Tell me. · Say it to him.

Cheat sheet

1. Ask "where", not "which preposition"
Point, surface or volume? The answer hands you at, on or in — in place and in time alike.
2. Movement means an arrow
Where? — in, on, at. Where to? — into, onto, to. Where from? — out of, off, from.
3. How long, or starting when
A length — for. A start point — since. A named event — during.
4. A deadline or a process up to it
Get it done by a moment — by. Keep going until a moment — until.
5. Two or many
Two named items — between. An unnamed mass — among.
6. After a verb, do not reason — recall
depend on, wait for, listen to, look at: these are dictionary pairs, not logic.
7. Check whether the preposition is surplus
home, tomorrow, next week, discuss, enter, call — no preposition belongs here.
8. After any preposition, a verb takes -ing
without saying, instead of going, good at swimming — never the infinitive.

And finally. Prepositions are the one part of English grammar where the logic runs out sooner than the list does. The images on this page cover most of what you will meet; the rest comes with reading. Every time you hit an unfamiliar verb-plus-preposition pair, write it down whole — never in halves.
