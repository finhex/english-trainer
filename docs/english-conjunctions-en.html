The Conjunction Handbook

A conjunction is a joining word — but "joining" hides three completely different mechanisms. Coordinating conjunctions link equals; correlative ones work in pairs; subordinating ones hook a dependent clause onto a main one. This handbook takes every conjunction in English one at a time — what it joins, the relationship it signals, its register, and the errors it causes — with an example for each.

Coordinating — join equals Correlative — in pairs Subordinating — embed a clause Conjunctive adverbs — the look-alikes
Part 0

What a conjunction actually is

A conjunction joins words, phrases, or clauses. But there are three real kinds — plus two families that look like conjunctions and aren't. Knowing which kind you're using decides both the grammar and the punctuation.

Coordinating — joins two units of equal rank: two words, two phrases, or two independent clauses (bread and butter; I called, but no one answered). Seven of them: FANBOYS.

Correlative — a coordinating idea split into two parts that bracket the joined items (both tea and coffee; either now or never).

Subordinating — hooks a dependent clause onto a main one, creating an unequal relationship (We left because it was late). The dependent clause cannot stand alone.

The asymmetry test tells coordinating from subordinating: delete one side. Delete a coordinated half and the other is still a whole sentence (I'm tired ~~and I want to sleep~~ → I'm tired.). Delete the main clause of a subordinate pair and what's left dangles (~~I stayed~~ because I was tired → ✗ because I was tired).

The two look-alikes. Conjunctive adverbs (however, therefore, moreover) feel like conjunctions but are adverbs — they need a full stop or semicolon, not a comma (Part 11). And a preposition (despite, because of) takes a noun, never a clause — which is why it's although it rained (conjunction + clause) but despite the rain (preposition + noun).

Part 1

Coordinating conjunctions — the seven (FANBOYS)

They join equals. When one links two full clauses, put a comma before it; when it links two words or phrases, no comma. Every coordinated pair must be grammatically parallel.

andaddition

The plain "plus". Beyond simple addition it also signals sequence (one thing then the next), result / consequence (do this and that follows), and commentary. It demands parallel items and, in a list of three or more, an optional Oxford comma before the final and.

  • bread and butter (addition)
  • He came in and sat down. (sequence)
  • Touch it and you'll get burned. (result — like a hidden "if")
  • red, white, and blue (Oxford comma optional)
Note: try and = try to (informal); go and see is idiomatic. Starting a sentence with And is fine for effect in modern usage.
butcontrast / exception

Signals that what follows runs against expectation. It also means except (everyone but me), forms the correction frame not X but Y, and appears in but for (= if it weren't for).

  • poor but happy (contrast)
  • I tried, but it failed. (clause contrast — comma before)
  • Everyone but me left. (= except)
  • It was not luck but skill. (not X but Y)
  • But for your help, I'd have failed. (= if not for)
oralternative

Offers an alternative or a choice; after an imperative it becomes a warning (= otherwise). It can be inclusive ("either or both") or exclusive ("one, not both") — context decides. Pairs with either.

  • tea or coffee? (choice)
  • Hurry, or we'll miss it. (= otherwise)
  • coffee, tea, or juice (list)
nornegative addition

Adds a second negative. After it, the clause inverts (auxiliary before subject), like a question. Usually follows a negative clause or pairs with neither.

  • I don't smoke, nor does she. (inversion)
  • He didn't call, nor did he write.
forreason (literary)

A formal / literary coordinator meaning because / seeing that. It joins two clauses as equals and always comes second, after a comma. Distinct from the preposition for (purpose, benefit).

  • He was glad, for the sun had come out.
  • We must act now, for time is short.
yetcontrast (emphatic)

Like but, but sharper — it stresses that the second fact is surprising given the first. Also functions as an adverb ("still / up to now").

  • simple yet effective
  • She's strict yet fair.
  • It's hard, yet not impossible.
soresult

Introduces a result / consequence of the previous clause. Keep it apart from the subordinator so that (purpose) and the degree frame so … that (result-degree).

  • It was late, so we left. (result)
  • vs so that: She left early so that she'd catch the train. (purpose)
Part 2

Correlative conjunctions — the pairs

These work in two parts that bracket the joined items. The iron rule is parallelism: whatever grammatical form follows the first half must follow the second.

both … anddouble addition

Emphasises that two things are true. Takes a plural verb.

  • Both the book and the film are good.
either … orchoice of two

One of two alternatives. The verb agrees with the nearer subject.

  • Either the manager or the staff are lying.
neither … nordouble negative

Not one and not the other. Already negative — don't add another not. Verb agrees with the nearer subject.

  • Neither he nor she knows.
not only … but alsoemphatic addition

Adds a second, often more striking, fact. Fronting not only triggers inversion.

  • She's not only clever but also kind.
  • Not only did she win, but she set a record. (fronted → inversion)
whether … oralternatives

Presents two possibilities, often "regardless which". Note whether … or not.

  • I don't know whether to laugh or cry.
  • We'll go whether it rains or not.
as … asequality

States two things are equal on some scale; negated as not as/so … as.

  • She's as tall as her brother.
  • It's not as cold as yesterday.
no sooner … than · hardly / scarcely … whenimmediate sequence

One event immediately follows another in the past. Fronting forces inversion and the past perfect.

  • No sooner had he sat down than the phone rang.
  • Hardly had we left when it started to rain.
so … that · such … thatresult of a degree

so + adjective/adverb + that; such + (a) + noun + that. Both express a result that follows from an extreme degree.

  • It was so cold that the pipes froze.
  • It was such a cold night that the pipes froze.
the … theproportional change

Two quantities changing together. Comma between the halves.

  • The harder you try, the luckier you get.
Part 3

Subordinating conjunctions — how they work

A subordinator opens a dependent clause and states its relationship to the main clause: time, reason, condition, concession, purpose, result, manner, place. The clause it makes can go first (with a comma) or last (usually none). Parts 4–9 take each relationship in turn.

The mechanism is simple — subordinator + subject + verb, hooked onto a main clause. The difficulty is the vocabulary: several high-frequency subordinators do two unrelated jobs (since, as, while), and English gives no formal signal for which is meant — Part 10 disambiguates them.

Position & comma. Front the subordinate clause for emphasis or scene-setting and mark the join with a comma (When the bell rang, everyone stood); put it last and the comma usually disappears (Everyone stood when the bell rang).

Part 4

Subordinators of time

They locate one event relative to another. The golden rule: no future tense inside a time clause — use the present even for future meaning.

whenat the time that

The general time link. No future in the clause: when I arrive, not ✗ when I will arrive.

  • I'll call you when I arrive.
  • When I was young, we walked to school.
while · asduring / simultaneous

Two things happening at once. while can also mark contrast (Part 7); as also marks parallel change and reason (Part 10).

  • While she read, I cooked.
  • As I was leaving, the phone rang.
before · aftersequence

Order two events. With after, a perfect tense can stress "finished first".

  • Wash your hands before you eat.
  • We left after we had eaten.
until · tillup to the point that

Marks the end-point of an action. till is the informal spelling.

  • Wait until the light turns green.
sincefrom a past point

Time meaning: from a starting point up to now — pairs with the present perfect. (Also = reason, Part 5/10.)

  • I've known her since we were children.
as soon as · once · the moment · by the time · whenever · now thattime, refined

as soon as / the moment = immediately when; once = as soon as / after; by the time = not later than (often + perfect); whenever = every time; now that = time + reason ("given that … now").

  • Call me as soon as you land.
  • Once you finish, you can go.
  • By the time we arrived, it had ended.
  • Whenever it rains, the roof leaks.
  • Now that you're here, let's start.
Part 5

Subordinators of reason & cause

They answer why?. The choice among them is about how you present the reason — as new information or as already known.

becausethe reason, focal

Presents the reason as the new, main point; usually comes second; can be the whole answer to why?. To attach to a noun, it becomes because of.

  • We stayed because it was late.
  • Cancelled because of the rain. (+ noun)
since · asthe reason, known

Present the reason as already known / background; often come first. Both are ambiguous (since = time, as = time/manner), so avoid where a sentence could be misread.

  • Since you're here, help me.
  • As it's late, let's stop.
for · seeing (that) · in that · inasmuch asreason, formal/literary

for = literary "because" (coordinator); seeing that = "given that" (informal); in that = "in the respect that" (formal); inasmuch as = "to the extent that" (very formal).

  • He smiled, for he had won.
  • Seeing that it's late, we'll stop.
  • It's odd in that no one noticed.
Part 6

Subordinators of condition

They set up a situation the main clause depends on. Rule: no will in the condition clause of a real future condition.

ifcondition

The core conditional word (zero / first / second / third). Distinct from whether (a choice / embedded yes-no question).

  • If it rains, we'll stay in.
  • If I had time, I'd help. (unreal)
unless= if not (single exception)

Names the one condition that would prevent the main clause. Not always interchangeable with if not.

  • I won't go unless you come.
provided (that) · providing · as long as · so long as · on condition thatstrong stipulation

= "only if this requirement is met". More emphatic than plain if.

  • You can go, provided that you finish.
  • I'll help as long as you're honest.
in case · supposing · given (that) · assuming (that)precaution / hypothesis

in case = as a precaution (not a condition): Take a key in case I'm out = whether or not you need it. supposing / assuming open a hypothesis; given that = "since it's a fact that".

  • Take an umbrella in case it rains.
  • Supposing he says no, what then?
even ifcondition + concession

= "whether or not / regardless". Hypothetical (contrast with even though, a real fact — Part 7).

  • I'll go even if it rains.
Part 7

Subordinators of concession & contrast

Concession grants a point that makes the main clause surprising; contrast simply sets two facts side by side. The words overlap.

although · thoughconcession

Introduce a fact that makes the main clause unexpected. although is neutral / slightly formal; though is the everyday equivalent and can also end a clause as an adverb.

  • Although he tried, he failed.
  • It's hard. I'll try, though. (end-position, informal)
even thoughstrong concession (real)

Emphasises a genuinely surprising, real fact. Contrast with even if (hypothetical).

  • Even though it was pouring, we went.
while · whereas · whilstdirect contrast

Set two clauses against each other. whereas is the pure-contrast word; while doubles as time; whilst is British/formal.

  • She's calm, whereas he panics.
  • While some agree, others don't.
much as · granted (that) · notwithstanding thatconcession, formal

much as = "although … a lot" (Much as I like it, I can't buy it); granted that concedes before objecting; notwithstanding that = "despite the fact that" (very formal).

  • Much as I'd love to, I can't.
Preposition, not conjunction: despite and in spite of mean the same but take a noun: ✗ despite it rained → despite the rain / despite the fact that it rained.
Part 8

Subordinators of purpose & result

Purpose looks forward to an intended aim; result looks back at what actually happened. They are easy to confuse because both can use so.

so that · in order thatpurpose (finite)

Introduce an aim, with a modal matching the tense: present/future takes can/will; past takes could/would. in order that is more formal.

  • I'll write it down so that I won't forget.
  • She spoke slowly so that everyone could follow.
lest · for fear that · in casenegative purpose

= "so that … not / to avoid". lest is formal/archaic and takes the subjunctive or should.

  • He whispered lest he be heard.
  • Write it down in case you forget.
so … that · such … thatresult of a degree

Express the effect of an extreme degree — the actual outcome, not an aim.

  • He was so tired that he fell asleep standing up.
  • It was such a mess that we gave up.
Purpose vs result in one pair: She saved money so that she could travel (purpose — the aim) vs She saved so much that she could travel (result — the effect).
Part 9

Subordinators of manner, comparison & place

Smaller groups, but each has a trap.

as · the waymanner

= "in the manner that". the way is the everyday equivalent. (Use as, not like, before a clause: Do as I say, not ✗ like I say in careful English.)

  • Do it as I showed you.
  • Leave it the way it was.
as if · as thoughunreal comparison

Introduce an imagined comparison; take an unreal past when hypothetical.

  • He talks as if he knew everything. (he doesn't)
thancomparison

Joins the second half of a comparison; the clause after it is trimmed by ellipsis (taller than I [am] → than me informally).

  • He earns more than she does.
where · whereverplace

Locate the main clause in space. wherever = "no matter where / any place".

  • Stay where you are.
  • Sit wherever you like.
Part 10

The hardest conjunctions, in full depth

A handful of words carry most of English's structural weight — and each does several unrelated jobs with no formal signal for which. These deserve a full entry apiece: every sense, every pattern, the register, and the errors.

thatthe most complex word in the grammar

that has four grammatical lives, and its conjunction life alone has several sub-rules. As a complementiser (a subordinating conjunction) it introduces a content clause — a whole clause acting as a noun — and carries no meaning of its own; it is a pure grammatical marker that says "a clause starts here".

When you can drop it. In an informal object position after a common reporting verb, that is optional: I think (that) it works · She said (that) she'd come.

When you must keep it. (1) When the clause is the subject: That he lied is obvious. (2) After a noun (an appositive content clause): the fact that he lied · the idea that time is money · the claim that it's safe. (3) After many adjectives in careful writing: aware that, confident that, afraid that. (4) When words come between the verb and the clause. (5) Whenever dropping it would blur where the clause begins.

Every slot the that-clause fills: subject (That she agreed surprised me), object (I know that…), subject complement (The trouble is that we're late), appositive (the fact that…), and extraposed subject with anticipatory it (It's clear that he lied). After verbs and adjectives of demand/importance the clause verb goes subjunctive: I insist that he be told.

Its other three lives (for contrast): relative pronoun with a gap, restrictive only (the book that I read __); the result correlative (so cold that · such a mess that); and the demonstrative, which is not a conjunction at all (that book · I want that). Fixed conjunction frames built on it: so that (purpose), now that (reason + time), in that (formal "because"), seeing that, given that.

  • complementiser (drop): I hope (that) you're well.
  • complementiser (keep — subject): That it failed shocked no one.
  • after a noun: the news that he'd resigned spread fast.
  • relative (gap): the car that she bought __
  • result: I was so tired that I slept for ten hours.
The gap test tells complementiser from relative: a relative clause is missing a noun (that I read __); a content clause is complete (that he lied). Missing piece → relative; complete → complementiser.
becausereason, in full

The core reason conjunction, answering why?. It presents its reason as the new, focal information — which is why a because-clause normally comes second and can stand as the whole answer to a question (Why? — Because I said so). It attaches to a full clause (because + subject + verb).

because vs because of. To give the reason as a noun, switch to the two-word preposition because of: ✗ because the rain → because of the rain / because it rained.

because vs since / as / for. because foregrounds the reason as the point; since / as treat it as already known background (and are ambiguous with time); for is a literary coordinator. Only because can be the sole answer to why?.

Starting a sentence with Because is perfectly correct when the main clause follows: Because it was late, we left. It is only a fragment when it stands alone with no main clause. And avoid the redundant the reason is because… — write the reason is that….

Scope ambiguity. I didn't leave because I was angry has two meanings: "I didn't leave, and the reason was anger" or "I left, but not because of anger." Intonation or a comma disambiguates in speech; rewrite it in writing.

Useful frames & register. just because … doesn't mean … (Just because it's legal doesn't mean it's right); the playful internet usage because + noun (because science) is nonstandard and jokey.

  • focal reason: We cancelled because the road was flooded.
  • + noun: We cancelled because of the flood.
  • fronted (fine): Because she'd trained hard, she won.
  • frame: Just because you can doesn't mean you should.
asfive jobs

The most overloaded connector. Reason (As it's late, let's stop), time / simultaneity (As I left, it rang), parallel change (As the sun rose, the fog lifted), manner (Do as I say), and comparison (as white as snow). A sixth use is really a preposition: role/capacity + noun (She works as a nurse = she is one; contrast like a nurse = merely resembling). Because reason and time share the word, avoid as wherever both readings fit; as before a clause is also the careful-English alternative to colloquial like (Do as I say, not ✗ like I say).

sincetime or reason

Two unrelated jobs. Time = from a past starting point up to now, and it pulls the present perfect (I've known her since 2010). Reason = a known cause, often first (Since you're here, help me). A genuinely ambiguous sentence is easy to build (Since you mentioned it, I've been worried — time or reason?). Force one reading with ever since (time) or given that (reason); the present perfect nudges toward time but doesn't guarantee it.

while · whilsttime or contrast

Time = during, simultaneous (While she read, I cooked); contrast = whereas (While some agree, others don't). whilst is the British/formal spelling, contrast-only in feel. A fronted While… is more often contrast; a mid-sentence one more often time.

if · whethercondition vs alternative

if sets up a condition (the whole conditional system — zero/first/second/third), with the key rule no will in the if-clause of a real future condition (if it rains). whether presents a choice between alternatives or an embedded yes-no question. They overlap only as the object of a verb (I asked if/whether it was true). Everywhere else only whether works — as a subject, after a preposition, and before to (whether or not to go). Related: even if (concession), as if (unreal comparison), if only (wish).

though · although · even thoughconcession, compared

All grant a point that makes the main clause surprising. although is neutral / slightly formal; though is the everyday twin and, uniquely, can end a clause as an afterthought adverb (It's hard. I'll try, though.); even though stresses a genuinely surprising real fact. Keep even though (real) apart from even if (hypothetical). And remember the concession preposition pair despite / in spite of takes a noun, not a clause.

for · thanthe two part-time conjunctions

for is a coordinator of reason only in literary style (He smiled, for he had won); everywhere else it's a preposition (+ noun). than joins the second half of a comparison, and the clause after it is trimmed by ellipsis, which is why it wavers between conjunction (taller than I am) and preposition (taller than me) depending on how much of the clause you keep.

Part 11

Conjunctive adverbs — the false conjunctions

These signal the same relationships (contrast, cause, addition) but are adverbs, not conjunctions. That single fact changes the punctuation: they cannot join two clauses with a comma.

The list, by relationship: addition — also, moreover, furthermore, in addition, besides; contrast — however, nevertheless, nonetheless, still, instead, on the other hand; result — therefore, thus, hence, consequently, accordingly; sequence — then, meanwhile, finally, subsequently; example — for example, for instance, namely; emphasis — indeed, in fact.

The punctuation rule. Two clauses around a conjunctive adverb need a full stop or a semicolon, not a comma. A comma there is the classic comma splice.

  • ✗ It rained, however we went. (comma splice)
  • ✓ It rained; however, we went.
  • ✓ It rained. However, we went.
  • ✓ It rained, but we went. (a real coordinator — comma is fine)

They are also mobile: a conjunctive adverb can sit at the front, in the middle, or at the end of its clause (We went, however. / We, however, went.) — a real conjunction is locked at the join.

Part 13

Common mistakes

Nearly all of them come from mixing up the four types, or from the conjunction-vs-preposition line.

  • Comma splice with a conjunctive adverb✗ I was late, however I came. → ✓ …late; however, I came.
  • Preposition used as a conjunction✗ despite it rained → ✓ although it rained / despite the rain
  • because of vs because✗ because the rain → ✓ because of the rain / because it rained
  • Double negative with neither…nor✗ I don't like neither. → ✓ I like neither.
  • Broken parallelism in a correlative✗ not only sings but also she dances → ✓ not only sings but also dances
  • will inside an if/time clause✗ if it will rain → ✓ if it rains
  • if where whether is needed✗ depends on if → ✓ depends on whether
  • Fragment — a subordinate clause alone✗ Because it was late. → ✓ We left because it was late.
  • like for as before a clause✗ like I said → ✓ as I said (careful English)
  • Missing inversion after fronted nor/negative✗ nor she does → ✓ nor does she

Some words are both a preposition and a conjunction — the same spelling takes a noun in one sentence and a clause in another: after, before, until, since, as. The one rule to leave with. Decide the type before the word: a coordinator joins equals (comma before, with clauses); a subordinator opens a dependent clause (comma only when it comes first); a conjunctive adverb links separate sentences (semicolon or full stop before it); a connecting preposition takes a noun, never a clause. Get the type right and both the grammar and the punctuation fall into place.

Part 15

The reporting-passive frames — It is said that…

A special construction built on a that-clause: it lets you state something as commonly held without personally vouching for it. That is exactly why it saturates news writing and academic hedging — and why it belongs in a full account of that.

Two structures, same meaning

  • Structure 1 — It + be + V3 + that-clauseIt is said that he is rich. · It was reported that the minister resigned.
  • Structure 2 — Subject + be + V3 + to-infinitiveHe is said to be rich. · The minister is reported to have resigned.

Both report the same fact impersonally. Structure 1 keeps the reported clause whole after that; structure 2 raises the subject to the front and turns the rest into an infinitive.

Time in structure 2 — the infinitive carries it

An infinitive has no tense, so English signals when through its form: a plain infinitive = same time as the reporting; a perfect infinitive = earlier.

Time relationInfinitive formExample
Same time as reportingto + baseHe is said to be rich.
Earlier than reportingto have + V3He is said to have been rich.
In progress nowto be + -ingHe is said to be working abroad.
In progress, earlierto have been + -ingHe is said to have been working there for years.

The reporting verbs that fit

say, think, believe, know, report, consider, claim, expect, suppose, allege, understand, feel, hold, presume, assume, estimate, fear, hope, argue, maintain, acknowledge, declare, prove, recommend, agree.

Which structure to pick. Structure 2 sounds more fluent with the everyday verbs (say, think, believe, report, expect, understand); structure 1 is the default for emotional-stance verbs (fear, hope) and official pronouncements (It is recommended that… · It has been agreed that…). After demand/recommendation verbs, structure 1 takes the subjunctive: It is recommended that he be present.

  • It is believed that the painting is genuine. → The painting is believed to be genuine.
  • She is alleged to have stolen the money.
  • The bridge is estimated to have cost millions.
  • It is feared that the hikers may not survive. (structure 1 more natural with fear)
