16 tenses · 4 aspects · 3 reference points

The English Timeline

Every English tense answers two questions at once: when did it happen, and how are you looking at it — as a fact, as a process, or as a result. Three answers to the first question multiplied by four answers to the second is why there are twelve tenses, not three. Each one gets a diagram showing exactly which part of the timeline it occupies.

Simple — a fact Continuous — a process Perfect — a result before a point Perfect Continuous — duration up to a point
Part 0 00:00

Reading the diagrams

All sixteen diagrams on this page are drawn with the same handful of marks. Spend a minute here and every tense below will read at a glance.

The axis
Time runs left to right: past on the left, future on the right.
Red dashes — now
The moment of speaking. The main reference point: every Present tense is measured against it.
A dot
A whole action seen as a fact. How long it took does not matter — only that it happened.
A bar
An action with length — it has an inside. Something else can happen while it runs.
A row of dots
Repeated, habitual or always true. The individual occasions do not matter — the pattern does.
An arc
A link: the action happened earlier but matters for a later point. That is what Perfect means.
A flag
A deadline or boundary: "by this moment". Every Perfect tense is measured from one.
A shaded zone
The past or future half of the axis — a hint about which side of "now" you are on.

One idea explains half of all tense mistakes. In most languages the form of the verb answers a single question: when? English verbs answer two at once — when, and how you are viewing the action: as a complete fact, as a process in progress, or as a result that stands at some later point. Pick the wrong view and the sentence is still grammatical but says something you did not mean.

The reference point is the second key idea. Present tenses are measured from now; Past tenses from a moment in the past; Future tenses from a moment in the future. Once you know where the reference point stands, all that is left is choosing the aspect.

Part 1 02:10

How the system works: 4 × 3

Do not learn twelve tenses as twelve separate rules. Learn four aspects and three reference points, and any tense assembles itself from two parts.

The aspect is your view of the action. It is set by the auxiliary verb and the form of the main verb:

Aspect Present · measured from now Past · measured from a past point Future · measured from a future point
Simplea fact, seen whole
V / V-s

Continuousa process, seen from inside

Perfecta result at a point

Building any tense takes two steps. First, choose the reference point — now, a moment in the past, or a moment in the future. That gives you the auxiliary: have/am → had/was → will have/will be. Second, choose the aspect — that gives you the form of the main verb: bare form, -ing, third form (V3), or been + -ing.

V3 is the past participle: write → wrote → written, work → worked → worked. On its own it is not a tense. It appears only alongside have / had / will have, and it means "done".

Word order never changes. The first auxiliary is the one that carries person and tense; everything after it is frozen. In a question the first auxiliary moves to the front; in a negative, not goes straight after it. Nothing else in the sentence moves.

Part 2 · Present 05:40

Reference point: now

Four tenses whose red dashes sit in the middle of the axis. All of them talk about the moment of speaking — but each looks at it differently: one never touches it, one sits right on it, one throws an arc towards it from the past.

Simple · Present · 01

Present Simple

generally, always, usually
+I / you / we / they work
+he / she / it works
−do not (don't) / does not (doesn't) work
?Do you work? · Does he work?
What it means

This is the one tense that does not point at a moment. It describes not an event but a property of the world or of a person: this is how things are, this is what happens, this is what repeats.

Hence the classic trap. I work in Moscow does not mean "I am working at this second" — it means "Moscow is where my job is", even if you are lying on a beach as you say it.

  • Regular, repeated actions and habits
  • Permanent states and characteristics
  • General truths, laws of nature, facts
  • Timetables and schedules — even about the future
  • State verbs (know, want, love, need), which almost never take Continuous
alwaysusuallyoftensometimesrarelyneverevery dayon Mondaystwice a week
Sentences
  • She drinks coffee every morning.
    A habit. Right now she may well be asleep.
  • Water boils at 100 degrees.
    Always true — dots across the entire axis.
  • I don't understand this rule.
    A state, not a process: "understanding" is not used here.
  • The train leaves at six tomorrow.
    A timetable: future meaning, present form.
  • Does your brother live here?
    In questions does takes the -s and the main verb goes bare.
Continuous · Present · 02

Present Continuous

in progress right now
+I am working
+he is / we are working
−am not / is not (isn't) / are not (aren't) working
?Are you working? · Is she working?
What it means

The bar can be narrow — literally this second (I am reading now) — or wide, covering this period of your life (I am learning Spanish this year). In the second case you may be asleep as you speak: what matters is that the process is under way and unfinished.

The second meaning is temporariness. Continuous says "this won't last"; Simple says "this is what I am like". Hence the difference between He is rude (he is a rude person) and He is being rude (he is behaving badly today).

  • An action at the moment of speaking
  • A temporary situation with a limited span
  • A changing, developing situation
  • A firm arrangement in the future
  • With always — irritation at something repeated
nowright nowat the momentcurrentlytodaythis weekLook!Listen!
Sentences
  • Be quiet, the baby is sleeping.
    The process runs exactly at the moment of speaking.
  • I am reading a great book this month.
    A wide bar: the book is shut right now, but the process is unfinished.
  • The climate is getting warmer.
    Slow change — also an unfinished process.
  • We are meeting the lawyer on Friday.
    An arrangement: time and place are already fixed.
  • He is always losing his keys!
    Not a fact but an emotion — irritation.
Perfect · Present · 03

Present Perfect

already done, and it matters now
+I / you / we / they have done
+he / she / it has done
−have not (haven't) / has not (hasn't) done
?Have you done? · Has he done?
What it means

Despite the name, this is not a past tense. Grammatically it is present: the auxiliary have is in the present. The sentence is about the current state of affairs, and the past action is only its cause.

Hence the iron rule: you cannot name a finished past time with Present Perfect. Not I have seen him yesterday — the moment you pin the action to a point on the axis, the link with "now" snaps and you need Past Simple.

Three typical meanings: a result you can see now; experience over a lifetime up to now; a period that has not finished yet (today, this week, this year).

  • A present result: something has changed
  • Life experience with no date attached
  • An unfinished time period
  • Something that has just happened — with just
  • Something still expected — with yet, already
justalreadyyeteverneverso farrecentlythis weeksince 2019for two years
Sentences
  • I have lost my passport.
    Lost at some point — what matters is that it is missing now.
  • She has never been to Japan.
    Experience across a whole life up to this minute.
  • We have finished three chapters today.
    "Today" is not over — the period is still open.
  • The train has just left.
    A fresh result: you have missed it.
  • Have you read the contract yet?
    Asking not "when" but "is it done".
Perfect Continuous · Present · 04

Present Perfect Continuous

it has been going on for a while
+I have been working
+he has been working
−haven't / hasn't been working
?Have you been working?
What it means

It answers the question "how long?". Present Perfect says "done"; Perfect Continuous says "has been going on for two hours". There is almost always a for (how long) or a since (starting when).

The second use is explaining visible evidence. You are soaked because you have been running; your eyes are red because you have been crying. The action may have just stopped, but its traces are on show.

State verbs (know, be, have, love) do not go into this form: it is I have known her for years, never have been knowing.

  • An action that began in the past and still continues
  • Answering "how long has this been going on?"
  • A recently finished action with visible traces
  • Emphasised duration, sometimes with a hint of tiredness or annoyance
for an hoursince morningall dayHow long...?latelyrecently
Sentences
  • I have been waiting for you for an hour.
    The bar runs right into "now" — that is why English needs a perfect here.
  • She has been working here since 2019.
    since marks where the bar starts.
  • Your eyes are red. Have you been crying?
    The action has stopped, but the evidence is visible.
  • How long have you been learning English?
    "How long" is this tense's home question.
Part 3 · Past 14:20

The reference point moves into the past

Here the red dashes stop doing any work. Everything is measured from a moment in the past, marked on the diagrams by a grey vertical. The auxiliaries simply shift into their past forms: am/is → was, are → were, have/has → had. The logic of the aspects does not change at all.

Simple · Past · 05

Past Simple

it happened then and it ended
+I worked · I went
−did not (didn't) work / go
?Did you work? · Did he go?
!after did — the bare verb
What it means

The default tense for any story about the past. The action is completely closed: it happened inside a finished stretch of time and has no bearing on the present.

A chain of Past Simples is a sequence of events, one after another, in the order written. That is why all narrative prose rests on this tense.

Its second use is habits that are over: I played football at school.

  • A single finished action with a stated time
  • A chain of events in a narrative
  • Former habits and states
  • A past fact whose present consequences are not the topic
yesterdaylast weektwo days agoin 2010when I was youngthen
Sentences
  • I bought this car in 2019.
    There is a date — so it can only be Past Simple.
  • She opened the door, looked inside and left.
    Three dots in a row — the order of events.
  • We didn't know about the meeting.
    didn't carries the past; know stays bare.
  • Did you call him last night?
    A question about a specific past moment.
Continuous · Past · 06

Past Continuous

it was in progress at that moment
+I / he was working
+we / you / they were working
−wasn't / weren't working
?Were you working?
What it means

This tense almost never lives alone — it needs a second coordinate: either a clock time (at 7 o'clock) or another action in Past Simple (when he came).

Remember the pair of conjunctions: while introduces the long action (the bar), when introduces the short one (the dot). While I was cooking, the phone rang.

If both actions are in Past Continuous, they ran in parallel — two bars side by side.

  • A process at a specific past moment
  • Background for a short event
  • Two simultaneous processes
  • Setting the scene at the start of a story
at 5 o'clockwhilewhen...all eveningat that moment
Sentences
  • At eight o'clock I was having dinner.
    The point on the axis is given — the process covers it.
  • While she was driving, it started to rain.
    while + the bar, with the event inside it.
  • They were arguing when I walked in.
    The argument began before I arrived.
  • He was cooking while I was cleaning.
    Two parallel bars.
Perfect · Past · 07

Past Perfect

it was already done before that moment
+I / he / they had done
−had not (hadn't) done
?Had you done?
!had — one form for every person
What it means

Past Perfect never stands on its own — it is always relative to another past point. No second point, stated or implied, means no Past Perfect: use plain Past Simple.

You need it exactly when the order of the words does not match the order of events. "The train left when I arrived" — if the leaving came first, that verb goes into Past Perfect.

Its other home is reported speech: both Present Perfect and Past Simple turn into Past Perfect when you report them.

  • An action earlier than another past action
  • The cause of a past situation
  • Reported speech and backshifting
  • After before, after, by the time, because
by that timebeforeafteralreadyby 2010when I arrived
Sentences
  • When I arrived, the train had already left.
    The leaving came before the arriving.
  • She had never seen the sea before that trip.
    Experience up to a past point — Present Perfect shifted backwards.
  • He was tired because he had worked all night.
    The cause precedes the effect.
  • She said she had lost the keys.
    Her actual words were: I have lost the keys.
Perfect Continuous · Past · 08

Past Perfect Continuous

it had been going on for a while by then
+I had been working
−hadn't been working
?Had you been working?
What it means

It answers "how long had this been going on by that moment". There is nearly always a for or a since, plus a second past point.

Its second use is explaining something visible in the past: the ground was wet because it had been raining.

Rare in speech, but regular in written texts and exams.

  • Duration up to a moment in the past
  • The cause of something visible in the past
  • Reported speech in place of Present Perfect Continuous
for two hourssince morningbefore thatall dayby then
Sentences
  • He had been driving for six hours when he stopped.
    The bar is measured up to the point "he stopped".
  • The ground was wet — it had been raining.
    Visible evidence in the past.
  • She had been waiting since noon, so she was angry.
    since sets where the bar starts.
Part 4 · Future 22:00

The reference point moves right of "now"

will is added to the front of the whole structure and it slides into the right half of the axis. After will comes the bare verb, so the chains get long — but they assemble mechanically: will be doing, will have done, will have been doing.

Simple · Future · 09

Future Simple

it will happen
+I / he / they will work
−will not (won't) work
?Will you work?
!will — one form for every person
What it means

will carries an important flavour: it is a decision made at the moment of speaking, a prediction, or a promise. If the plan was ready beforehand, English prefers be going to or even Present Continuous.

I'll help you — decided just now, on seeing you. I'm going to help you — you had already intended to.

The trap: after if, when, as soon as, until the future is not used — the present goes there instead: When he comes, I will tell him.

  • A spontaneous decision made as you speak
  • A prediction or opinion about the future (I think, probably)
  • A promise, a refusal, an offer of help
  • Inevitable events outside anyone's control
tomorrownext yearin an hoursoonI thinkprobablyone day
Sentences
  • I think it will rain tomorrow.
    A forecast, not a plan.
  • That bag looks heavy — I will help you.
    The decision was born at this moment.
  • She won't tell anyone, I promise.
    won't also means "refuses to".
  • When he arrives, we will start.
    Present after when, even though the meaning is future.
Continuous · Future · 10

Future Continuous

it will be in progress at that moment
+I will be working
−won't be working
?Will you be working?
What it means

Use it when it matters that you will catch the action mid-flow. Not "I'll do it at nine" but "at nine I'll be busy with it".

The second meaning is unexpected: it is the politest way to ask about someone's plans. Will you be using the car? sounds like idle curiosity, whereas Will you use the car? can sound like pressure.

  • A process at a specific future moment
  • Something that will happen in the natural course of events
  • A polite enquiry about someone's plans
at 10 tomorrowthis time next weekall day tomorrowwhen you arrive
Sentences
  • At nine tomorrow I will be flying to Berlin.
    A dot inside the bar.
  • Don't call at seven — we will be having dinner.
    Explaining why that moment is taken.
  • Will you be using the printer later?
    Polite: just checking your plans.
Perfect · Future · 11

Future Perfect

it will be done by the deadline
+I will have done
−won't have done
?Will you have done?
What it means

The key word is by: by Friday, by the time you come. It is by that plants the flag the readiness is measured against. Without a deadline this tense is not used.

Compare: I will finish at six — I'll finish exactly at six. I will have finished by six — by six it will already be done, possibly earlier.

  • An action completed before a set future moment
  • Totting things up at a deadline ("by then it will be ten years")
  • A confident assumption about something already done
by Fridayby thenby the time...before you comein two years
Sentences
  • I will have finished the report by Friday.
    Friday is the flag; the work ends before it.
  • By the time you arrive, we will have eaten.
    Present tense after by the time.
  • Next June they will have been married for ten years.
    A total at a deadline.
Perfect Continuous · Future · 12

Future Perfect Continuous

by then it will have been going on for N
+I will have been working
−won't have been working
?Will you have been working?
What it means

The rarest tense in the language. It needs two conditions at once: a future deadline (by…) and a duration (for…). The bar usually starts back in the past and crosses "now" on its way.

Worth knowing for reading and exams rather than for conversation: in real speech people almost always use Future Perfect instead.

  • Duration of an action up to a future moment
  • Emphasis on the unbroken process rather than its result
by 2030 ... for 20 yearsby thenwhen you finish
Sentences
  • By May I will have been working here for five years.
    by is the flag, for is the length of the bar.
  • When you arrive, I will have been driving for ten hours.
    The flag is set by an event, not a date.
Part 5 · Future in the Past 29:10

Future in the Past: the four forgotten tenses

These are why the count is sixteen rather than twelve. The idea is simple: we stand in the past and look forward from there. For the narrator the event is already over; for the character in the story it is still ahead. The mechanics are one substitution: will → would. Everything else works as in the Future.

Where you meet them. First and foremost in reported speech: I will call you becomes He said he would call me. Then in any narrative about the past where you need to say "and this is what lay ahead of him". And finally in second and third conditionals, where would lives permanently.

Simple · Future in the Past · 13

Future Simple in the Past

it was going to happen — looking forward from the past
+I / he would work
−would not (wouldn't) work
?Would you work?
What it means

The dot can land anywhere relative to "now" — that is not the point. All that matters is that it is later than the reference point in the past.

Do not confuse it with would meaning "used to": We would go there every summer is a past habit, a different use of the same word.

  • Reported speech in place of will
  • Intentions and expectations of a character in a story
  • Second conditionals
he said (that)...she knew...the next daya week later
Sentences
  • He said he would call me the next day.
    His words were: I will call you tomorrow.
  • We knew the meeting would be difficult.
    We knew it then — the meeting lay ahead.
  • She promised she wouldn't tell anyone.
    A promise made in the past.
Continuous · Future in the Past · 14

Future Continuous in the Past

would be in progress — seen from the past
+I would be working
−wouldn't be working
?Would you be working?
What it means

The same Future Continuous, reported from the past. Mostly found in reported speech and in narrative fiction.

  • Reported speech in place of will be doing
  • An expected process in the character's future
he said...at that time the next day
Sentences
  • She said she would be waiting at the station.
    Her words: I will be waiting.
  • I knew they would be sleeping at that hour.
    A process that would cover that future moment.
Perfect · Future in the Past · 15

Future Perfect in the Past

would be done by the deadline — seen from the past
+I would have done
−wouldn't have done
?Would you have done?
What it means

The very same form — would have done — also does the work of the third conditional, about an unreal past: If I had known, I would have come. Tell them apart by context: reported speech, or regret about something that never happened.

  • Reported speech in place of will have done
  • Third conditionals (an unreal past)
he said... by Fridayif I had known...
Sentences
  • He said he would have finished by Monday.
    His words: I will have finished by Monday.
  • If I had known, I would have helped you.
    Third conditional: the past cannot be changed now.

So rare that many native speakers produce it a handful of times in a lifetime. It is enough to recognise it in a text and understand what it says: a duration counted up to a deadline that was in the future of a past moment.

Part 6 34:30

Pairs people confuse

Mistakes almost never happen between distant tenses. They happen inside five pairs — and each pair differs by exactly one feature on the timeline.

Present Simple or Present Continuous

The question is not "when" but "in general or right now". A permanent property against a temporary process.

Present Simple — in general
She works in a bank.
Banking is her profession. The moment of speaking is irrelevant — she could be on holiday.
Present Continuous — right now
She is working in a bank this month.
Temporarily, just this month. After that it will be something else.

Past Simple or Present Perfect

The single most important pair in English. The test is simple: is there a time label on the axis? If the time is stated or understood as closed, use Past Simple. If only today's result matters, use Present Perfect.

Past Simple — WHEN matters
I lost my keys yesterday.
Pinned to yesterday. I may have found them since — the sentence says nothing about that.
Present Perfect — the RESULT matters
I have lost my keys.
When is neither stated nor relevant. What counts is that I have no keys now.

Present Perfect or Present Perfect Continuous

Both reach "now". The difference is what you are measuring: how much got done or how much time was spent.

Perfect — how much is done
I have written three letters.
The result is counted: three letters exist.
Perfect Continuous — how long it took
I have been writing letters all morning.
How many is unknown. What is known is that the process ran all morning.

Past Simple or Past Perfect

Both are entirely in the past. Past Perfect is needed only when you must show that one thing happened before another and the word order does not show it.

Both Past Simple — in sequence
When I arrived, she left.
First I arrived, then she left. The events run in a chain.
Past Perfect — earlier
When I arrived, she had left.
She left before I got there — I missed her.

Three ways to talk about the future

There is one future on the timeline but three forms — and the choice shows not the time but how ready the plan is.

will — decided now
OK, I'll call him.
A spontaneous decision, a prediction, a promise. There was no plan a second ago.
be going to — intention
I'm going to call him tonight.
The decision predates the conversation. Also used for visible evidence: Look at the clouds — it's going to rain.
Present Continuous — arranged
I'm meeting him at six.
More than an intention: time and place are fixed and the other person knows.

Time signals

These words plant a point on the axis by themselves, and they often identify the tense faster than the meaning does. But remember: a signal is a hint, not a verdict — the meaning decides.

Part 8 44:20

Cheat sheet: eight questions to ask yourself

When you need to pick a tense, run these questions in order — they get you there faster than sorting through all sixteen forms.

1. Where is my reference point?
Now → the Present group. A past moment → Past. A future moment → Future. The past, looking forward → would.
2. Is a finished past time named?
If yes (yesterday, in 2019, ago) — Past Simple, and no have/has anywhere.
3. Does the result matter right now?
Yes, and no time is stated — Present Perfect. Test: you can slip in the word "already".
4. Is there a "how long"?
for / since / How long — take Perfect Continuous at the right reference point.
5. Is the action mid-flow at a stated moment?
A moment is given — so Continuous: am/was/will be + V-ing.
6. Did one thing happen before another?
The earlier one goes into Perfect (had done / will have done); the later one into Simple.
7. Is it a state verb?
know, want, love, need, believe, belong hardly ever take Continuous — use Simple or Perfect.
8. Are you reporting someone's words?
Shift everything one step back: will → would, do → did, have done → had done.

And finally. In real speech nine sentences out of ten fit into five tenses: Present Simple, Present Continuous, Present Perfect, Past Simple and Future Simple. Get those five to the point of reflex — the other eleven you will first recognise in other people's speech, and then one day produce without thinking.

Part 9 48:00

Inside a native speaker's head

A native doesn't run through rules. In the half-second before speaking they ask two silent questions — where am I standing in time? and how do I see the action — as a whole, in progress, or as a result? — and the form falls out. Here is that inner monologue, slowed down, on the sentences learners find hardest.

Situation. There's something you keep planning to do and still haven't.
It started in the past and it's still true now — I need a tie back to now, so: perfect.
But it isn't a finished result — it's an ongoing, unfinished intention. So: continuous.
Perfect + continuous, anchored at now → have been + -ing.
Out loudI've been meaning to call you.
Situation. Two things happened in the past and you want to show which came first.
Both are past. The one I mention isn't the anchor — our arrival is the past reference point.
The other event happened before that point, so I push it one step further back → had + V3.
already confirms it: the leaving was complete by the time we got there.
Out loudThe train had already left when we arrived.
Situation. A deadline in the future, and you want to say the job will be done in time.
I mentally stand at the future moment — by nine — and look back from there.
From that spot the action is already finished: a result at a future point → will have + V3.
Out loudBy nine I'll have finished the report.
Situation. You explain why a past state was the way it was — the cause behind it.
The red eyes are the past scene. The cause happened even earlier and lasted — before that scene.
Earlier-than-past + duration → past perfect continuous, had been + -ing.
Out loudHer eyes were red — she'd been crying.
Situation. You report what someone told you earlier.
The saying happened in the past, so the whole quoted world shifts one step back with it.
will → would, is → was, have done → had done. That backshift is the signal it's reported, not live.
Out loudHe said he would come. → (from "I will come.")
Situation. Same three words, two different pictures — for ten years.
If she still lives here, the ten years reach up to now → present perfect: She's lived here for ten years.
If she has moved away, the ten years are a closed, finished box → past simple: She lived here for ten years.
The tense isn't decorating the sentence — it's telling the listener whether she's still your neighbour.
The pointThe form carries the fact. Choose it on purpose.

Situations that force a tense

Forget the words for a moment and look at the situation. Each of these real-life meanings pulls one tense toward it almost automatically. The italic line is what a native is really thinking.

An action earlier than another past action → Past Perfect
When I arrived, the meeting had already started.
Native's thought: "Two past events — I'll shove the first one a step deeper into the past so the order is unmistakable."
The cause of a past situation or state → Past Perfect / Past Perfect Continuous
The ground was wet; it had been raining. · He failed because he hadn't revised.
Native's thought: "The scene is in the past; the reason for it sits even further back — perfect handles 'before the past'."
Reporting speech — backshift one step → shift the tense back
"I am tired." → She said she was tired. · "I will help." → He said he would help. · "I have seen it." → They said they had seen it.
Native's thought: "The telling is already in the past, so everything inside the quote slides back with it: is→was, will→would, have→had."
After before, after, by the time, until → sequence, often Past Perfect
By the time the police came, the thief had disappeared. · She ate after she had washed her hands.
Native's thought: "These words already fix the order for me, so I mark the earlier action with had — or, if the order is obvious, I relax and use two past simples."
After because — giving a reason → match the timing of the cause
I'm tired because I've been working all day. · We were late because the train had been cancelled.
Native's thought: "because doesn't force one tense — I just ask when the cause happened relative to the result, and pick accordingly."
A finished action with a result you can see now → Present Perfect
I've lost my keys. (so I can't get in now) · She's broken her arm.
Native's thought: "I don't care when — I care that the result is standing here in front of me right now."
How long something has gone on — up to now → Present Perfect Continuous
We've been living here for five years. · How long have you been learning English?
Native's thought: "It's still going, and I'm measuring the stretch of it — for/since plus a live process."
One action interrupted by another in the past → Past Continuous + Past Simple
I was sleeping when the alarm went off.
Native's thought: "The long background is the continuous; the short thing that cuts into it is the simple."
A timetable / schedule in the future → Present Simple
The train leaves at 6. · The film starts at eight.
Native's thought: "It's fixed by a timetable, not by me — so I treat it like a present fact."
A plan or arrangement you've already made → Present Continuous
We're having dinner with them on Friday.
Native's thought: "It's settled, diary-fixed, with a person and a time — that's the present continuous, not will."
A prediction with evidence in front of you → going to
She's going to have a baby. · Watch out — you're going to drop it!
Native's thought: "I can see it coming from the here-and-now, so going to — will would sound like a guess out of nowhere."
A decision made at the moment of speaking → will
The phone's ringing — I'll get it. · Fine, I'll help you.
Native's thought: "I decided this half a second ago, on the spot — that's exactly what will is for."
An unreal or imagined past (if) → Third Conditional: had + V3 … would have + V3
If I'd known, I would have come.
Native's thought: "It didn't happen — I'm rewinding a past that never was, so both halves go one step past the past."

The habit to build. Before you speak, don't scan for a rule — scan the situation. Is the time closed or open? Is the action whole, in progress, or a result? Is one thing before another? Those three questions, asked in a blink, are what a fluent speaker is really doing, and they land you on the right tense far faster than the sixteen boxes ever could.
