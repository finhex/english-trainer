Sentence architecture · from one clause to the hardest

The Sentence Blueprint

Every English sentence, however long, is built from one small kit: a handful of roles (subject, verb, object, complement, adverbial), assembled into clauses, joined by two moves only — coordination (linking equals) and subordination (embedding one inside another). Learn the kit and the two moves, and even a page-long sentence becomes readable. This page goes from the smallest sentence to the hardest, missing nothing in between.

Subject Verb Object Complement Adverbial
Part 0

The atom: a subject and a predicate

The smallest complete sentence is two parts: a subject (who or what the sentence is about) and a predicate (what is said about it — always built around a verb). Everything else on this page is these two parts growing.

SubjectBirds
Predicate (verb)sing.

Two words, one complete thought. The subject names the topic; the predicate makes a claim about it.

The subject is usually a noun phrase — a noun and everything hanging off it: The old man next door is one subject, however many words it contains. The predicate is a verb phrase — the verb plus whatever the verb needs to feel complete.

The verb is the engine. It decides what the rest of the sentence must contain: some verbs need nothing (sing), some need an object (need a key), some need a description (seem tired). That demand is the single most useful thing to notice about any verb — Part 2 turns it into five fixed patterns.

Part 1

The five roles every clause is built from

English has only five grammatical roles. Master what each one is, and you can label any clause on Earth.

S — Subject V — Verb O — Object C — Complement A — Adverbial

Subject (S) — who or what does the action or is described. Normally before the verb: The engineers approved it.

Verb (V) — the action or state. The one role no clause can drop.

Object (O) — who or what receives the action. A direct object answers "what/whom?" (She wrote a letter); an indirect object is the receiver of the direct one (She wrote him a letter).

Complement (C) — completes the meaning by describing the subject or object, rather than receiving action. It follows linking verbs (be, seem, become, look): She is a doctor · He looks tired · They elected her chair (object complement).

Adverbial (A) — when, where, why, how, how often. Almost always optional, and movable: Yesterday she left quietly.

The crucial difference — object vs complement. An object is a different thing from the subject (She saw a doctor — two people); a complement is the same thing, renamed or described (She is a doctor — one person).

Part 2

The clause patterns: the verb decides the shape

Combine the five roles and you get just seven basic clause patterns. Which one you use is dictated entirely by the verb — this is the backbone of every English sentence.

PatternShapeExampleVerb type
IntransitiveS + VThe baby slept.needs nothing
TransitiveS + V + OShe wrote a letter.needs one object
DitransitiveS + V + O + OHe gave her a book.indirect + direct
LinkingS + V + CThey are happy.describes the subject
Complex-transitiveS + V + O + CWe painted the door red.describes the object
Intransitive + adverbialS + V + AShe lives in Rome.needs a place/adverbial
Transitive + adverbialS + V + O + AHe put the keys on the table.object + obligatory adverbial

Why this matters more than any rule. The verb carries its pattern with it, like a plug shape. put demands an object and a place (✗ He put the keys is broken); sleep refuses an object (✗ She slept the baby); give happily takes two. When a sentence feels wrong, the verb's pattern is usually the reason.

Part 3

Phrases vs clauses — the key distinction

A phrase is a group of words acting as one unit but with no subject–verb pair. A clause has a subject and a verb of its own. Everything about sentence-building comes down to knowing which you are looking at.

Phrase typeBuilt aroundExample
Noun phrase (NP)a nounthe tall man in the grey coat
Verb phrase (VP)a verbhas been working
Adjective phrase (AdjP)an adjectivevery proud of her
Adverb phrase (AdvP)an adverbquite slowly
Prepositional phrase (PP)a prepositionin the morning

The test. in the morning is a phrase — no verb. when the sun rose is a clause — it has a subject (the sun) and a verb (rose). Both can do the same job (tell you when), but only the clause contains a mini-sentence inside it. Growing a sentence means slotting clauses into the roles of Part 1.

Part 4

Finite vs non-finite clauses

Clauses split into two families by their verb. This split explains half of the "hard" sentences later.

Finite clause

verb shows tense + a real subject

The verb is marked for tense and agrees with a subject. A finite clause can stand as a full sentence (if independent).

She arrived late. · because he was tired

Non-finite clause

to-infinitive / -ing / -ed · often no subject

The verb shows no tense; the subject is usually left out and understood. It never stands alone — it always leans on a main clause.

To be honest, … · Feeling tired, she left. · the report written last week

Why it matters. Non-finite clauses are how English compresses. Because she felt tired, she left (finite) shrinks to Feeling tired, she left (non-finite) — same meaning, fewer words. Most "advanced" style is finite clauses collapsed into non-finite ones. Part 11 is devoted to them.

Part 5

Independent vs dependent clauses

A finite clause is one of two kinds — and the whole grammar of sentence types rests on the difference.

Independent (main) clause

stands alone as a sentence

Expresses a complete thought by itself. Every grammatical sentence has at least one.

The train left.

Dependent (subordinate) clause

opens with a subordinator; cannot stand alone

Begins with a word like because, when, if, that, which, who that hooks it onto a main clause. Alone it is a fragment.

✗ because the train left · ✓ We waited because the train left.

The fragment trap. When the lights went out. feels like a sentence — it has a subject and a verb — but the opening when leaves the thought hanging: then what? A dependent clause always needs a main clause to complete it.

Part 6

The four sentence types

Count the clauses and how they are joined, and every sentence in English is one of exactly four shapes.

Simple

one independent clause

A single main clause. It can still be long — one subject, one verb, plenty of phrases.

The tired old dog slept by the fire all afternoon.

Compound

two+ independent clauses, joined as equals

Main clauses linked by a coordinator (FANBOYS) or a semicolon. Neither outranks the other.

She knocked, but no one answered.

Complex

one independent + one+ dependent

A main clause with one or more subordinate clauses embedded in it.

She left because the train that she wanted had gone.

Compound-complex

two+ independent + one+ dependent

The two previous moves at once — the workhorse of real writing.

She left because the train had gone, and she walked home.

The whole game in one line. There are only two ways to put clauses together: coordinate them (link equals — Part 7) or subordinate one (embed it as a role inside another — Parts 8–10). Every sentence, up to the longest, is these two moves used repeatedly.

Part 7

Coordination: joining equals

Coordination links two units of the same rank — two clauses, two objects, two adjectives. The joiners are few and worth memorising.

The seven coordinators — FANBOYS

For (reason), And (addition), Nor (negative addition), But (contrast), Or (alternative), Yet (contrast), So (result). When one joins two full clauses, put a comma before it: It was late, so we left.

Correlative pairs

both … and, either … or, neither … nor, not only … but also, whether … or. They must bracket parallel items: ✗ She not only sings but also she dances → ✓ She not only sings but also dances.

Conjunctive adverbs are not coordinators

however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, thus feel like joiners but are adverbs. Two clauses around one need a semicolon, not a comma: It rained; however, we went. Using a comma there is the classic comma splice (Part 14).

The Native Mental Trigger — coordinate or subordinate?

Before joining two ideas, a native asks: are these two equal, side-by-side facts, or is one the main point and the other a condition, cause, or detail attached to it? Equal weight → coordinate with and / but / so. One serving the other → subordinate it with because / although / when / which.

The same two facts, weighted two ways
  • Coordinated — equal billingIt was raining, and we stayed in.
  • Subordinated — the rain is just the reasonBecause it was raining, we stayed in.
Part 8

Subordinate clause #1 — noun clauses

A subordinate clause does one of three jobs: it acts as a noun, an adjective, or an adverb. First the noun clause — a whole clause filling a slot where a single noun could sit.

A noun clause can be the subject, the object, or the complement of a bigger clause. It opens with that, a wh-word (what, who, why, how, where, when), or whether / if.

Subject = a clauseWhat he said
Verbsurprised
Objecteveryone.

The whole clause What he said is the subject — swap it for the pronoun it and the frame still holds.

  • Object of a verb (that-clause)I know that she's right.
  • Object — the that is often droppedI know she's right.
  • wh- clause as objectTell me what you want.
  • whether / if — a yes-no question embeddedI wonder whether it's true.
  • Complement after beThe problem is that we're late.
  • After a preposition (only wh-/whether)It depends on what he decides.

Extraposition — the "it" trick. A clause subject at the front feels top-heavy, so English usually shoves it to the end and puts a placeholder it in front: That he lied is obvious → It is obvious that he lied. The it means nothing; it just holds the subject slot.

Reported speech is noun clauses. "I'm tired," she said → She said that she was tired. The quoted words become a that-clause object, and the tense backshifts one step.

Deep dive — the mechanism, the slots, and the traps

What a noun clause really is. Take any statement or question and freeze it into a single lump that behaves like a noun: He is late → that he is late; Where is it? → where it is. That lump can now drop into any slot a noun phrase could fill. The clue is always the same: could you replace the whole thing with it or the answer and keep a grammatical frame? If yes, it's a noun clause.

Every slot it can fill — with a sentence for each.

  • SubjectWhat he decided shocked us.
  • Direct object of a verbI know that you're right.
  • Subject complement (after be)The trouble is that we're broke.
  • Object of a preposition (wh-/whether only)It depends on what you want.
  • In apposition to an abstract nounthe fact that he lied · the idea that time is money
  • Delayed subject with anticipatory itIt's clear that he lied.

The three introducers, deeply.

that marks a statement / fact. It is droppable in an informal object slot (I think (that) it works) but must stay when the clause is the subject (That he lied is obvious), after certain nouns and adjectives in careful writing, and whenever dropping it would blur where the clause begins.

wh-words (what, who, which, whose, where, when, why, how) embed a wh-question — but the moment the question is embedded it loses its question shape: statement word order, no do-support, no inversion, no question mark. Where is he? → I don't know where he is. (✗ where is he). This single rule fixes the most common advanced error.

whether / if embed a yes/no question. whether is the flexible one — it can be the subject, follow a preposition, sit before a to-infinitive, and take or not (whether or not to go). if is restricted: it works only as the object of a verb (I asked if it was true), never as a subject, never after a preposition, never before to.

Content clauses vs relative clauses — a subtle but real difference. In the fact that he lied, the that-clause is a noun clause naming the fact — it has no gap. In the lie that he told, the that-clause is a relative clause with a gap (he told __). Same word, two different jobs: content clause = renames the noun; relative clause = describes it and is missing a piece.

The subjunctive noun clause. After verbs and adjectives of demand, suggestion, or importance, the that-clause verb goes into the bare base form (mandative subjunctive): I insist that he be told · It's vital that she arrive early. British English often prefers should: …that he should be told.

The classic errors. (1) Leaving question order in an embedded wh-clause: ✗ Tell me what is your name → what your name is. (2) Using if as a subject or after a preposition: ✗ It depends on if… → on whether…. (3) Dropping that where the clause is the subject or where meaning would blur.

Part 9

Subordinate clause #2 — relative (adjective) clauses

A relative clause does the job of an adjective: it sits right after a noun and tells you which one or adds information about it. It is the single most powerful tool for packing detail into a sentence.

Nounthe man
Relative clause (adjective job)who fixed my car

The clause who fixed my car modifies the man exactly as an adjective would.

The relative words

who / whom (people), which (things), that (people or things, defining only), whose (possession), and the relative adverbs where, when, why.

Defining vs non-defining — the comma changes the meaning

  • Defining — identifies which one, no commasThe book that I lent you is overdue.
  • Non-defining — extra info, commas, never thatThis book, which I love, is overdue.

Why the comma is not decoration. My brother who lives in Rome (defining) implies I have several brothers and mean the Roman one. My brother, who lives in Rome, (non-defining) implies I have one brother, and mention Rome as an aside. The punctuation carries real information.

Dropping the relative pronoun

When the relative word is the object of its clause, you can drop it: the book (that) I lent you. When it is the subject, you cannot: the man who fixed my car (✗ the man fixed my car loses the join).

Prepositions in relative clauses

Everyday English leaves the preposition at the end and drops the pronoun: the house (that) we lived in. Formal English pulls it to the front with which/whom: the house in which we lived.

Reduced relative clauses

A relative clause often collapses to a phrase. the parcel that was left at the door → the parcel left at the door (past participle). the man who is waiting → the man waiting (-ing). This is why long noun phrases so often end in -ing or -ed — they are relative clauses with the who/which + be squeezed out.

Deep dive — the mechanism, the whole system, and the traps

What a relative clause really is. Take two sentences that share a noun: I read the book. + The book won a prize. To fold the second into the first, you delete the repeated noun, leave a gap where it stood, and put a relative word at the front to mark the join: the book [which __ won a prize]. That gap is the key — a relative clause is always a full clause with exactly one noun phrase missing, and the relative word is standing in for the missing piece.

The role of the gap decides everything. Whether you use who or whom, and whether you can drop the word at all, depends on what job the missing noun did inside its own clause:

  • Gap = subject → pronoun requiredthe man who __ called (✗ the man called)
  • Gap = object → pronoun droppablethe man (who/whom/that) I called __
  • Gap = object of a prepositionthe house (that) we lived in __ / in which we lived
  • Gap = possessor → whosethe poet whose work __ we studied
  • Gap = adverbial → relative adverbthe day when we met / the place where we met

The pronoun grid. who = a personal subject; whom = a personal object (formal — everyday English uses who or zero); which = a thing; that = person or thing but only in restrictive clauses; whose = possessor of person or thing (a house whose roof leaks is fine); zero (nothing) = an object relative in a restrictive clause; where / when / why = a relative adverb, each equal to preposition + which (the year when = the year in which).

Restrictive vs non-restrictive — the deep reason. A restrictive clause is part of naming the thing: without it you would not know which one, so it fuses to the noun with no comma and can use that or zero. A non-restrictive clause is a parenthetical aside about an already-identified thing: it takes commas, is spoken with a drop in the voice, can be lifted out without changing who you mean, and never uses that or zero — only who/whom/which/whose. Test: if you could put the clause in brackets and still know the referent, it is non-restrictive.

  • Restrictive — identifies, no commasStudents who cheat will fail. (only the cheaters)
  • Non-restrictive — comment, commasMy father, who cheats at cards, is charming. (extra info)

Sentential (connective) relatives. A non-restrictive which can refer back to a whole clause, not a single noun: He arrived late, which annoyed everyone. Here which = "the fact that he arrived late." This is the one time which has no noun antecedent.

Prepositions — stranding vs pied-piping. Everyday English leaves the preposition stranded at the end of the clause and drops the pronoun (the people (that) I work with); formal English "pied-pipes" the preposition to the front with which/whom (the people with whom I work). You may pied-pipe only with which/whom, never with that (✗ with that I work) and never while dropping the pronoun.

Quantified relatives (formal). A quantifier can sit in front of whom / which: forty guests, many of whom were strangers · two options, neither of which is cheap · the twins, the elder of whom is a doctor.

Reduction — why they collapse. A relative clause whose verb is be can drop who/which + be, leaving a participle or phrase: the parcel [that was] left at the door (passive, -ed); the man [who is] waiting (active, -ing); the person [who is] responsible (adjective phrase); and a special active case, the first man to arrive (to-infinitive, after ordinals/superlatives/the only).

The classic errors. (1) A comma before a restrictive clause: ✗ The book, that I need, is here. (2) that in a non-restrictive clause: ✗ My car, that is red, … → which. (3) A resumptive pronoun filling the gap that the relative already fills: ✗ the man that I saw him → drop him. (4) which for a person: prefer who.

Part 10

Subordinate clause #3 — adverbial clauses

An adverbial clause does an adverb's job on the whole main clause: it tells when, where, why, despite what, on what condition. It is introduced by a subordinating conjunction, and it can usually move to the front (with a comma) or the end (without).

MeaningSubordinatorsExample
Timewhen, while, as, before, after, until, since, as soon as, by the timeAs soon as she arrived, we ate.
Conditionif, unless, provided (that), as long as, in caseWe'll go unless it rains.
Concession (contrast)although, though, even though, while, whereasAlthough he tried, he failed.
Reason / causebecause, since, as, seeing thatWe stayed because it was late.
Purposeso that, in order that, so as to, toShe whispered so that no one would hear.
Resultso … that, such … thatIt was so cold that the pipes froze.
Manner / comparisonas, as if, as though, than, the … theHe talks as if he knew everything.
Placewhere, whereverSit wherever you like.

Front or end — and the comma. Put the adverbial clause first for emphasis or set-up, and mark the join with a comma: When the bell rang, everyone stood. Put it last and the comma usually disappears: Everyone stood when the bell rang.

Conditionals are adverbial clauses of condition. The whole conditional system (zero, first, second, third, mixed) is just an if-clause plus a main clause, with the verb forms shifted to mark how real or unreal the situation is — see the tenses page for the timeline behind them.

Deep dive — each type, its tense rule, and worked sentences

Time clauses — the golden rule: no future after a time word. Even when the meaning is future, the verb inside a time clause stays present. ✗ when I will arrive → I'll call you when I arrive. Use the present perfect for "finished first": We'll leave after we have eaten. With since, the main clause takes the present perfect: I've known her since we were children. while / as mark simultaneity (As I was leaving, the phone rang); by the time pairs with a perfect (By the time you read this, I'll have gone).

Conditional clauses — real vs unreal, the whole ladder.

  • Zero — general truth (present + present)If you heat ice, it melts.
  • First — real future (present → will)If it rains, we'll stay in.
  • Second — unreal present (past → would)If I had time, I'd help.
  • Third — unreal past (past perfect → would have)If I'd known, I'd have come.
  • Mixed — past cause, present resultIf I'd studied medicine, I'd be a doctor now.

unless ≈ if not for the sole exception (I won't go unless you come); provided / as long as add a strong stipulation; in case is precaution, not condition (Take an umbrella in case it rains = as a precaution, whether or not it does). Formal English drops if and inverts: Had I known… · Were I you… · Should you need help…

Concession clauses — contrast against expectation. although / though / even though introduce a fact that makes the main clause surprising: Although he's rich, he's unhappy. though can also end a sentence as an adverb (It's hard. I'll try, though.). Distinguish even though (a real fact: even though it was raining) from even if (a hypothetical: even if it rains). whereas / while mark direct contrast between two clauses (She's calm, whereas he panics).

Reason clauses — three flavours. because gives the reason as the new, focal information and usually comes second (We left because it was late); since / as present the reason as already known and often come first (Since you're here, help me). Only because can answer a direct why?.

Purpose clauses — finite and non-finite. Finite purpose uses so that + a modal, matching the tense: present/future takes can/will (I'll write it down so that I won't forget); past takes could/would (She whispered so that no one would hear). Non-finite purpose uses to / in order to / so as to (He left early to catch the train) — shorter, and the default in speech.

Result clauses — the finished effect. so + adjective/adverb + that (It was so cold that the pipes froze); such + (a) + noun + that (It was such a cold night that the pipes froze). Purpose looks forward to an intention; result looks back at what actually happened.

Manner & comparison. as if / as though take an unreal past when the comparison is imaginary: He talks as if he knew everything (he doesn't). The correlative the …er, the …er links two moving quantities: The harder you try, the luckier you get.

Reduced adverbial clauses. When the subject matches the main clause, the subject and be can drop: When [you are] in doubt, ask · While [I was] waiting, I read · Once [it is] finished, send it.

Part 11

Non-finite & verbless clauses — how English compresses

These are clauses with no tensed verb and usually no stated subject. They let you fold a whole clause into a phrase, and they are everywhere in fluent writing.

  • to-infinitive clause (purpose, etc.)She stopped to buy milk.
  • -ing clause (simultaneous action, reason)Feeling tired, he sat down.
  • -ed (past-participle) clause (passive sense)Shaken by the news, she left.
  • verbless clause (a phrase standing in for a clause)When in doubt, ask.
  • absolute construction (its own subject)The work finished, we went home.

The dangling-participle danger. A non-finite clause borrows its missing subject from the nearest noun — usually the subject of the main clause. If they don't match, the sentence says something absurd: ✗ Walking to school, the rain started. (the rain wasn't walking) → ✓ Walking to school, I got caught in the rain. Always check that the hidden subject of the -ing/-ed clause is the one you mean.

Deep dive — the three forms, the hidden subject, and every use

Why they exist. A non-finite clause is a finite clause with the tensed verb and (usually) the subject stripped out, so it can be slotted into a bigger sentence as compactly as a phrase. The reader recovers the missing subject from context — normally the subject of the main clause. That recovery is called control, and getting it wrong is the dangling-participle error.

1 · to-infinitive clauses — every use, with a sentence.

  • Purpose (= in order to)She left early to catch the train.
  • After an adjectiveIt's hard to explain. · I'm glad to help.
  • As the subject (formal)To give up now would be foolish.
  • After too / enoughIt's too hot to touch. · old enough to vote.
  • With its own subject: for + object + to-infI'm waiting for him to reply.
  • After certain verbs (catenative)They agreed to meet. · I want to know.

2 · -ing clauses — participial (adverbial / adjectival) and gerundial (noun).

  • Reason / cause (adverbial)Being tired, he went to bed.
  • Time / simultaneous actionWalking home, I met Sam.
  • Result, trailing the main clauseThe bomb exploded, killing two.
  • Post-modifier (reduced relative)the man standing at the door
  • Gerund as subject / object (noun job)Smoking is banned. · I enjoy reading.
  • Gerund with its own subject (possessive/objective)I dislike his/him arriving late.

3 · -ed (past-participle) clauses — always passive in sense.

  • Reason / circumstance (adverbial)Shaken by the news, she sat down.
  • Post-modifier (reduced passive relative)the report written last week
  • ConditionGiven more time, we'd finish.

4 · Verbless & absolute clauses. A verbless clause drops even the verb: When in Rome, do as the Romans do · Though exhausted, she smiled. An absolute clause keeps its own subject and so does not depend on the main clause for one: The weather being fine, we walked · Her work finished, she left · He stood there, arms folded. Because it carries its own subject, an absolute clause can never dangle.

The dangling trap, and the fix. A participial or infinitival clause with no stated subject grabs the subject of the main clause. If that noun cannot logically do the action, the sentence is broken: ✗ Driving home, the deer ran out. (the deer wasn't driving). Three fixes: give the participle the right main subject (Driving home, I saw a deer run out); give the non-finite clause its own subject (As I was driving home, the deer ran out); or rewrite as two clauses.

Part 12

Combining & embedding: growing a real sentence

Long sentences are not a different grammar — they are the same clauses nested and chained. Here is how a native builds one up, and how to keep it readable.

Start with the spine — the one main clause that carries the point:

Main clause (the spine)The report was rejected.

Hang subordinate clauses onto its roles — a relative clause on the noun, an adverbial on the whole thing:

noun + relative clauseThe report that we submitted
main verbwas rejected
adverbial clausebecause it missed the deadline.

Combine two short sentences — the six moves

Start with two bare facts: The car was old. + It broke down. Every way of joining them is one move from this page — pick the one whose weighting you want.

  • Coordinate (result) — equal weightThe car was old, so it broke down.
  • Subordinate (reason) — one serves the otherBecause the car was old, it broke down.
  • Relative clause — fold one onto the nounThe car, which was old, broke down.
  • Reduced relative — drop which + beThe old car broke down.
  • -ing participle clause — compress the causeBeing old, the car broke down.
  • Adverbial of time — a different linkThe old car finally broke down when it hit the hill.

Three sentences folded into one

Short and choppy: The engineer was tired. She had worked all night. She still fixed the bug.

  • Although she was tired, the engineer, who had worked all night, still fixed the bug.
    How it's combined: the contrast becomes a concession clause (although she was tired), the middle fact becomes a non-defining relative on the engineer (who had worked all night), and the last fact stays as the spine (still fixed the bug). One complex sentence, three facts, each given its right weight.

Coordinate a second spine if the ideas are equal:

  • The report that we submitted was rejected because it missed the deadline, but the client agreed to a second attempt, which the team is now preparing.
    How it's built: two main clauses (was rejected · the client agreed) joined by but; each carries subordinate clauses — a relative (that we submitted), an adverbial (because it missed…), a purpose object (to a second attempt), and a trailing relative (which the team is now preparing). Compound-complex, yet every piece is a Part 1–10 unit.

Parallelism — the rule that keeps long sentences readable

Items joined by and / or / but must share the same grammatical form. Mismatched forms are the commonest sign of a sentence that has grown out of control: ✗ She likes reading, to swim, and cooked meals → ✓ She likes reading, swimming, and cooking.

When to stop

English tolerates deep nesting, but readers hold only so much. If a sentence forces the reader to remember an unfinished clause across many words (Part 13's centre-embedding), break it in two. Fluency is not maximum length — it is putting the main point where it can be found.

Part 13

The hardest structures — and how to read them

These are the constructions that make a sentence feel impossible. Each is just the ordinary kit rearranged for emphasis, focus, or economy. Learn the shape and the difficulty evaporates.

Cleft sentences — splitting one clause to spotlight a part

  • it-cleft (spotlights one element)It was John who broke it. (not anyone else)
  • wh-cleft / pseudo-cleftWhat I need is a holiday.

Extraposition — a heavy clause pushed to the end

To finish this today is impossible → It is impossible to finish this today. The it is an empty placeholder; the real subject waits at the back.

Fronting & inversion — moving a piece to the front for effect

Push an element to the front and, with negatives, flip subject and auxiliary: Never have I seen such a mess. · Not only did she win, but she also set a record. · Down the hill rolled the barrel.

Centre-embedding — a clause buried inside a clause inside a clause

  • The rumour that the manager the board hired had resigned spread quickly.
    How to read it: peel from the outside. Spine: The rumour … spread quickly. Inside: the rumour [that the manager … had resigned]. Inside that: the manager [(that) the board hired]. Three clauses nested at the same point — grammatical, but the reason writers avoid stacking them.

Garden-path sentences — grammatical, but they trick the parser

  • The horse raced past the barn fell.
    The trick: raced past the barn looks like the main verb, but it's a reduced relative — the horse [that was] raced past the barn — and fell is the real verb. You must re-read; that is the point.
  • The old man the boats.
    The trick: man here is the verb (to staff), the old is the subject (old people): The old [people] man [operate] the boats.

Apposition — a noun phrase renaming another

My neighbour, a retired pilot, flies drones. The middle phrase is not a clause; it simply renames my neighbour, set off by commas.

Ellipsis — leaving out what can be recovered

English deletes repeated material: She can sing and he can [sing] too → She can sing and he can too. · I'll help if I can [help]. The gap is understood from context.

Part 14

Punctuating clauses — the joints of a sentence

Punctuation marks the seams between clauses. Get these five right and most sentence errors disappear.

MarkJoinsExample
Comma + FANBOYStwo independent clausesIt was late, so we left.
Semicolontwo related independent clauses, no conjunctionIt was late; we left.
Colona clause that leads to an explanation or listShe had one goal: to finish.
Comma (front adverbial / non-defining)an introductory or extra clauseWhen it rang, he answered.
Dashan abrupt aside or afterthoughtHe agreed — reluctantly — to help.

The three classic sentence errors

  • Comma splice — two clauses, only a comma✗ It was late, we left. → ✓ It was late; we left.
  • Run-on / fused — no mark at all✗ It was late we left. → ✓ It was late, so we left.
  • Fragment — a dependent clause alone✗ Because it was late. → ✓ We left because it was late.
Part 15

Form vs function: the four sentence purposes

A separate axis from structure: what the sentence does. Form (how it's built) and function (what it's for) usually match — but not always, and the mismatch is where politeness and nuance live.

FunctionTypical formExample
Statement (declarative)S + V …The door is open.
Question (interrogative)aux + S + V / wh-Is the door open?
Command (imperative)V … (no subject)Open the door.
Exclamation (exclamative)What / How …!What a mess this is!

When form and function part ways. A question-shaped sentence can be a request (Could you open the door? = a command, politely), and a statement can be a question by intonation (You're leaving?). Native fluency is largely this gap — choosing a gentler form for a direct function.

Part 16

Build & check any sentence — the drill

A repeatable routine for writing a clear sentence and for unpicking a hard one.

To build

  • 1. Fix the spineOne main clause = the point. S + V + whatever the verb needs.
  • 2. Attach detail as clauseswhich/who (relative) on a noun; because/when/if (adverbial) on the whole.
  • 3. Decide equal vs subordinateEqual ideas → and/but/so. One serving another → subordinate it.
  • 4. Keep joined items parallelreading, swimming, cooking — not reading, to swim, cooked.
  • 5. Punctuate the seamscomma+FANBOYS, semicolon, front-comma; no comma splices.

To decode a hard sentence

  • 1. Find the main verbIgnore everything in commas/brackets first — locate the spine's verb.
  • 2. Name its subjectWho or what does that verb? That pair is the sentence's core claim.
  • 3. Bracket each subordinate clauseMark where each which/that/because/when clause starts and ends.
  • 4. Peel from the outside inRead the spine, then drop each clause back into its role.

The one idea to keep. There is no such thing as an "advanced" sentence — only the five roles, the two families of clause, and the two moves of joining them, used again and again. Every sentence you will ever meet is this kit, rearranged.

Appendix A1 · Deep dive

Verb complementation — what each verb allows after it

The clause pattern (Part 2) says whether a verb takes an object; complementation says what shape that object may be — a clause, a to-infinitive, an -ing form, a bare infinitive, or an object plus one of these. Getting this right is most of what separates natural English from translated English.

Verb takes…Typical verbsExample
that-clausethink, know, say, believe, hope, suggestI think that she's right.
to-infinitivewant, decide, hope, agree, refuse, manage, offerShe decided to leave.
-ing (gerund)enjoy, avoid, finish, mind, deny, suggest, keepHe avoided answering.
object + to-infinitivewant, tell, ask, expect, advise, allow, forceI want him to go.
object + bare infinitivemake, let, have; (perception) see, hear, feel, watchShe made him wait. · I saw her leave.
object + -ing (in progress)see, hear, watch, find, catch, keepI saw her running (mid-action).
wh-clauseknow, wonder, ask, decide, depend onI wonder what he wants.

The pairs where the choice changes the meaning

  • stop to do = pause in order to doHe stopped to smoke. (stopped so he could smoke)
  • stop doing = end the activityHe stopped smoking. (quit)
  • remember to do = the task is aheadRemember to lock the door.
  • remember doing = the memory is behindI remember locking the door.
  • try to do = attempt (may fail)I tried to open it. (couldn't)
  • try doing = experiment with a methodTry restarting it. (as a test)
  • regret to say = about to deliver bad newsI regret to inform you…
  • regret saying = sorry about a past actI regret saying that.
  • mean to do = intendI meant to call.
  • mean doing = involve / entailWinning means training daily.

Perception verbs — the two forms are not the same. I saw her cross the road (bare infinitive) = I saw the whole, completed action. I saw her crossing the road (-ing) = I saw it in progress, a slice of it. The grammar encodes whether you witnessed the event whole or mid-flow.

Appendix A2 · Deep dive

Subject–verb agreement in the hard cases

Agreement is trivial until a phrase, a quantifier, or a clause gets between the subject and the verb. These are the traps that survive into advanced writing.

  • An intervening phrase — agree with the head nounThe box of chocolates is empty. (box, not chocolates)
  • each / every / either / neither → singularEvery one of the players was ready.
  • a number of = many → pluralA number of people were late.
  • the number of = one figure → singularThe number of errors was small.
  • neither … nor → agree with the nearer subjectNeither the boss nor the workers were told.
  • Collective noun — sg. as a unit, pl. as membersThe team is strong. · The team are arguing.
  • there is / there are → agree with what followsThere are two problems.
  • A clause / gerund as subject → singularWhat we need is time. · Reading helps.
  • Relative-clause verb agrees with the antecedentShe's one of the people who were chosen.
  • Fossilised -s nouns (fields, diseases) → singularPhysics is hard. · The news is good.
Appendix A3 · Deep dive

Every kind of question

Questions are built by moving an auxiliary, adding a wh-word, or tacking on a tag. The one that trips learners is the difference between a direct question and an embedded one.

  • Yes/no — invert subject & auxiliaryAre you ready? · Did she call?
  • wh- object — needs doWho did you see? (you = subject)
  • wh- subject — no do, no inversionWho saw you? (who = subject)
  • Tag question — flip the polarityShe's late, isn't she? · You didn't, did you?
  • Negative question — surprise / expecting yesAren't you coming?
  • Embedded / indirect — NO inversionDo you know where it is? (✗ where is it)
  • Indirect yes/no — use if / whetherI asked whether she was ready.
  • Echo / rhetoricalYou did what? · Who cares?

The embedded-question rule in one line. Once a question sits inside another clause (after know, wonder, ask, tell me…), it stops being a question in shape: the subject and verb go back to normal order and the ? may vanish. Where is he? → I don't know where he is.

Appendix A4 · Deep dive

Negation & scope

Where not attaches, and how far its meaning reaches, changes what a sentence denies. Scope is the source of some genuinely ambiguous English.

  • not goes after the first auxiliaryShe has not finished. · I do not know.
  • no + noun = not anyThere's no milk. = There isn't any milk.
  • Negative raising — deny the main verb, mean the subI don't think he'll come. (= I think he won't)
  • any, not some, under negationI didn't buy any. (✗ some)
  • Fronted negative adverb → inversionNever have I seen it. · Not once did she complain.
  • Partial vs total negation (scope!)All that glitters is not gold. (= not all of it is)
  • Semi-negatives count as negativeHardly anyone came. · Seldom does he smile.

A worked ambiguity. I didn't leave because I was angry has two readings by scope: (a) I didn't leave — and the reason was my anger, or (b) I did leave, but not because of anger. Fluent speakers disambiguate with intonation or a comma; writers rebuild the sentence.

Appendix A5 · Deep dive

The subjunctive & other unreal forms

English keeps a small subjunctive for things demanded, wished, or imagined — and a set of inversions that replace if in formal style.

  • Mandative subjunctive (base form) after demand/suggestI insist that he be present. · It's essential she arrive on time.
  • were-subjunctive for the unrealIf I were you… · I wish I were taller.
  • wish + past / past perfectI wish I knew. · I wish I had known.
  • would rather / it's time + pastI'd rather you didn't. · It's time we left.
  • as if / as though + unreal pastHe talks as if he owned the place.
  • Inversion instead of if (formal)Had I known… · Were I you… · Should you need help…

Why the past tense means "unreal", not "past". In all of these, a past-tense form points not to past time but to distance from reality. If I knew is about now, imagined; the backward step in tense is the grammar's way of saying "this isn't the real world." The same mechanism drives the whole second/third-conditional system.

Appendix A6 · Deep dive

Information flow: why the hard structures exist at all

Almost every "advanced" construction on this page — the passive, clefts, extraposition, existential there, fronting — exists to serve one principle of how English packages information.

Two forces shape word order beyond grammar. First, given before new: start with what the listener already has in mind, end with the new point. Second, end-weight: put the long, heavy phrase last, because a heavy element at the front makes the listener hold too much before the verb arrives.

Every "hard" structure is one of these forces in action

  • Passive — move the topic to the front, doer to the backThe bridge was designed by a famous architect.
  • Extraposition — send the heavy clause to the endIt's odd that no one noticed.
  • Existential there — introduce something new at the endThere's a man at the door.
  • it-cleft — spotlight the new elementIt was the deadline that killed the project.
  • Fronting — put the linking (given) element firstThat question, I can't answer.
  • Dative shift — reorder by weight/newnessShe gave the museum her entire collection. (heavy last)

The unifying idea. English grammar fixes roles (subject before verb before object), but the speaker still chooses which real thing fills each role and what gets moved — and that choice is governed by keeping old news first and heavy news last. Learn to feel those two pulls and the "hard" structures stop being a list to memorise; they become the obvious tool for the job.

Appendix A7 · Deep dive

A gallery of the hardest sentences, fully parsed

Nothing here breaks a rule on this page — each is the ordinary kit pushed to its limit. Read the parse and every one becomes tractable.

  • The cheese the rat the cat chased ate was rotten.
    Triple centre-embedding. Spine: The cheese … was rotten. Inside: the cheese [(that) the rat … ate]. Inside that: the rat [(that) the cat chased]. Grammatical, nearly unreadable — proof that grammar allows more nesting than working memory does.
  • The complex houses married and single soldiers and their families.
    Garden path. You read houses as a noun; it's the verb. Subject = The complex (a housing complex); verb = houses (accommodates); object = married and single soldiers and their families.
  • The horse raced past the barn fell.
    Reduced-relative garden path. raced past the barn = [that was] raced past the barn, a reduced passive relative; the real main verb is fell.
  • More people have been to Russia than I have.
    Comparative illusion ("Escher sentence"). It sounds meaningful but isn't — there is no coherent quantity being compared. A reminder that fluency includes recognising a well-formed shell with no content.
  • Anyone who says they understand this sentence, which loops back on the very claim it makes, is trusting the grammar more than the meaning.
    Long but orderly. Spine: Anyone … is trusting the grammar more than the meaning. Subject Anyone carries a defining relative (who says they understand this sentence), which itself contains a noun-clause object ((that) they understand this sentence), and the sentence carries a non-defining relative aside (which loops back…). Deep, but every layer is a Part 8–10 unit.
  • Not until the results were published did the scale of the error become clear.
    Negative fronting + inversion. Fronting Not until… forces subject–auxiliary inversion: did the scale … become clear. Unfold it to the plain order: The scale of the error did not become clear until the results were published.

The closing rule. When a sentence resists you, do not look for a new grammar — find the main verb, name its subject, bracket every subordinate clause, and read from the outside in. On this page there is nothing more than five roles, two clause families, and two ways to join them. Everything else is these, arranged for effect.

Appendix A8 · Deep dive

Comparative & correlative constructions

Comparison has its own little grammar: a frame (as … as, -er … than, the … the) and a second clause that is almost always trimmed by ellipsis. These are structural, not just vocabulary.

  • Equality — as + adj/adv + asShe's as tall as her brother.
  • Inequality — not as / not so … asIt's not as cold as yesterday.
  • Comparative — -er / more … thanThis is more useful than that.
  • Comparison of clauses (than-clause)He earns more than she does.
  • Gradual change — double comparativeIt's getting colder and colder.
  • Proportional / correlative — the … theThe more you read, the more you know.
  • Result — so … that / such … thatIt was so good that I stayed.
  • Superlative + reduced relativethe best film (that) I've seen

Why the second half looks incomplete. The clause after than / as is a full clause with everything predictable deleted: She's taller than I [am tall] → She's taller than I am → than me (casual). That is why than behaves like a conjunction (than I am) and, informally, like a preposition (than me) — the difference is just how much of the clause you keep.

The correlative frame is a real clause pair. The harder you work, the more you earn is two clauses locked together, each opening with the + comparative fronted for the pattern; unfolded it means You earn more to the degree that you work harder. The comma between the two halves is obligatory.

Keep the two sides parallel. Whatever is compared must be grammatically matched: ✗ Driving is faster than to take the train → ✓ Driving is faster than taking the train. A comparison across mismatched forms is the commonest error in this whole area.

Appendix A9 · Deep dive

The order of adverbials

One adverbial anywhere is easy; the trouble starts when a sentence needs two or three at once. English sequences them by how tightly each binds to the verb — and getting this order wrong gives your English a foreign accent even when every word is right.

The M–P–T rule: Manner → Place → Time

When several adverbials pile up, the default order is manner, then place, then time. Manner sits closest to the verb (it's almost part of describing the action); place locates the event a step out; time frames the whole thing and sits furthest away.

  • manner → place → timeShe sang beautifully in the hall last night.
  • manner → place → timeHe worked hard at the office all weekend.

Movement verbs flip it — Place before Manner

With verbs of motion (go, come, travel, walk, drive, fly), the destination is close to the verb's core meaning, so place comes first and manner (the means) becomes the outer element.

  • place → manner (means)They travelled to London by train.
  • place → mannerHe walked home slowly.

Time can detach to the front

Because time is the loosest, most frame-setting element, it can peel off and move to the front to set the scene: Yesterday, I saw her. · Every summer, they rent a cottage.

Frequency & certainty adverbs take mid-position

A different class — always, often, never, usually, probably, definitely, rarely — comments on the verb itself, so it tucks in mid-sentence, not at the edges: before the main verb (I always drink coffee), after the first auxiliary (She has never been), and after be (He is probably late). Not at the start: ✗ Always I drink coffee.

The scope principle behind it all. The element most tightly bound to the verb sits closest; the element most detachable and broadest in scope sits furthest, and is exactly the one that can be fronted for emphasis. Same logic runs through adjective order before a noun (opinion furthest, purpose nearest).

Appendix A10 · Deep dive

Word order with phrasal verbs

Whether a particle can move away from its verb depends on the verb's type — but the moment the object is a pronoun, the particle is forced to the end. This is one of the fastest ways to sound native or not.

Separable — particle either side of a noun, but pronoun forces it to the end

  • Full noun object — either orderTurn on the light. / Turn the light on.
  • Pronoun object — particle must go last✓ Turn it on. · ✗ Turn on it.
  • Another pairFill in the form. / Fill it in. (✗ fill in it)

Why the pronoun rule is near-exceptionless. A pronoun is light and given (you only use one for something already identified). English pushes light, given material toward the front of a phrase and saves the end for heavier or newer content — so a pronoun stranded after the particle sits exactly where weight belongs, and speakers reject it outright.

Inseparable — the particle never leaves the verb

  • Particle behaves like a prepositionI ran into him. (✗ ran him into)
  • Even a pronoun follows the whole unitShe looked after her. (✗ looked her after)

Three-word (phrasal-prepositional) — always inseparable

  • Two particles = one unbreakable unitI can't put up with it. (✗ put it up with)
  • Object always follows the whole chainWe ran out of it. · She came up with one.

There's no perfect surface test for separable vs inseparable — learn it verb by verb — but a rough guide: a particle acting like a real preposition (look after somebody) resists separation, while one acting like an adverb of manner (turn on/off) allows it.

Appendix A11 · Deep dive

Comment clauses & small clauses

Two clause types that sit outside the main frame. A comment clause steps out of the claim to comment on it; a small clause is a subject–predicate pair with no verb of its own. Both are everywhere, and a text with no comment clauses sounds oddly robotic.

Comment (parenthetical) clauses — commenting on your own sentence

These are reduced main clauses (I think that… → I think) that float free and attach anywhere to signal how sure you are, how you want to be taken, or whether you expect agreement. Grouped by job:

  • Opinion (softens fact → belief)I think · I suppose · if you ask me · in my opinion
  • Hedging (weakens without retracting)so to speak · as it were · sort of · kind of
  • Engagement (invites the listener in)you know · you see · mind you · believe me
  • Certainty / stanceof course · no doubt · presumably · apparently · admittedly
  • Softening bad news (not doubt)I'm afraid we're out of coffee.

Position changes the effect — and each takes commas: I think he's the best. (frames from the start) · He's, I think, the best. (a mid-flow hedge, very conversational) · He's the best, I think. (an afterthought that softens a claim already made).

The test, and the trap. A comment clause is parenthetical — remove it and the sentence is still complete: He's, I think, the best → He's the best. That is why I think he's right with no comma is not a comment clause at all — it's an ordinary main clause I think plus a that-clause object. Only when I think moves off the front, or is clearly set off by commas, does it become a comment.

Small clauses — a subject and a predicate, but no verb

A small clause is a bare subject + predicate with no verb of its own, embedded whole inside a bigger clause. You have already met it as the S V O C pattern (Part 2): the object and its complement are a small clause.

  • object + adjective complementWe painted [the door red].
  • object + noun complementThey elected [her chair].
  • after consider / find / wantI consider [him a genius].
  • after with (attendant circumstance)He slept [with the window open].

The bracketed pair has its own subject–predicate relation (the door is red) but no tensed verb — which is exactly why it is a clause in meaning yet fits inside a single larger clause in form.
